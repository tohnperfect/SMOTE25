Dataset variant registry

One dataset name does not mean one dataset. Across the seven recovered characteristics tables (eight papers, since MC-CCR and MC-RBO share one), the same name is published with different instance counts, different class counts, different feature counts and different class distributions. Nothing here is merged or dropped. Every variant that any paper used is kept as its own row, with the papers that used it and a note saying how it differs. A survey that silently reconciles these would be reporting a dataset nobody ran.

Severity: variant means the data itself differs, so the rows must never be pooled. reporting means the data agrees but a published number does not follow from it. undefined means an IR was given with no formula stated.

Imbalance ratio: every definition in use

The corpus reports imbalance under at least nine different measures. None is dropped here. When a figure is quoted in the survey it carries the definition that produced it, because the same dataset scores 1.89 under one and 5.6 under another.

A six-axis taxonomy for the datasets in this literature

A single list of dataset names is not a classification. The axes below are orthogonal: any benchmark in the corpus receives one value on each. D1 is the provenance axis requested for the survey; D2 to D5 are the axes that actually separate papers once provenance is held fixed; D6 records how the imbalance came to exist, which turns out to be the axis most often left unstated. Counts beside each level are the number of papers in this corpus that touch it.

Use D1 and D6 together when reading any result table. Two papers can both report "20 UCI datasets, IR up to 175" and still be testing different things, because one inherited natural skew and the other manufactured it by undersampling a balanced source.

What the axes expose
  • Provenance is nearly uniform. UCI and KEEL together account for the overwhelming majority of evaluations, and a large share of the KEEL sets are themselves binarised UCI data, so the two repositories are far less independent than paper counts suggest.
  • Dataset counts are not comparable. The three largest benchmarks in the corpus reach their size by multiplying a smaller pool: 66 KEEL sets from 16 sources in RBBoost, 90 datasets from 12 UCI sources plus KEEL and MADELON in k-means SMOTE, 69 from 28 in G-SMOTE. A reader who ranks papers by dataset count ranks them by resampling policy.
  • The inclusion rule is usually missing. Only a handful state one: IR > 1.5 with a minimum minority size of 10 (AMDO), IR > 1.5 (OVO-SMOTE), IR > 10 and overlap R-value > 0.3 (MLOS), IR floor of about 8 (SplitBal), size below 1000 then random draw (KNNOR), and a preregistered protocol with a disjoint tuning suite (CISO, RBO).
  • Difficulty is asserted more often than measured. Class overlap is named by 30 papers but quantified per dataset by only two (SHSampler and GDHS, via Fisher discriminant ratio) plus MLOS (augmented R-value) and the Saez-based difficulty typing used by MC-CCR, MC-RBO and MC-NCLWO.
  • Multi-class has no agreed minority definition. Four incompatible rules coexist: smaller than the mean class size (SMOM, SCUT, SHSampler), a numeric imbalance-degree threshold (OREM-M at 1.2, MKC-SMOTE at 1.45), an explicit hand-made list (MDO, SOUP), and a data-difficulty test on top of relative size (GMMSampling). IR itself is reported under at least three incompatible definitions.
  • One name is not one dataset. Twenty of the seventy-five names in the recovered tables carry a conflict: a different source release (thyroid 7200 vs 720, flare 1066 vs 1389), a curation difference (dermatology 366 vs 358, cleveland 297 vs 303), a class subset (MKC-SMOTE uses 5 of ecoli's 8 classes, moving its IR from 71.50 to 4.93), or binarisation. Every variant is kept as its own row in the registry.
  • Within a lineage the benchmark is literally the same. MC-CCR and MC-RBO evaluate on an identical list of 20 KEEL datasets and MC-NCLWO on a strict superset of it, so D1 and D5 are constant across three papers from one group. MKC-SMOTE is the clearest break from the pattern, drawing 21 UCI sets that barely overlap the KEEL suite.
  • Scale is a blind spot. Outside SPE, GDO and a few deep-learning papers, the entire corpus evaluates on datasets that fit in memory on a laptop, typically under 10000 rows.

4. Data

Resampling research is empirical research. A new oversampler is accepted or rejected almost entirely on the strength of a table of benchmark results, so the datasets behind that table determine what the field can claim to know. This section characterises the experimental data used across the 69 primary studies reviewed here: how much data each study evaluates on, how the datasets were chosen, what properties they carry, and how they can be organised into a taxonomy that makes results from different papers comparable. Studies are counted per publication: the conference (2006) and journal (2010) versions of Global-CS are separate records because the journal version adds seven learner types, but they share one 20-dataset benchmark, so every dataset-level tally below counts that benchmark twice.

4.1 How much data does a study evaluate on?

The number of datasets per study spans more than two orders of magnitude. Nine studies evaluate on five or fewer, and five of those use two or fewer: Safe-Level-SMOTE reports results on two UCI sets, Cluster-SMOTE on two proprietary network-traffic captures, ClusterCentroids on two real datasets plus a synthetic family whose size is not stated, SHRINK on a single oil-spill dataset, and Tomek's paper on a single illustrative two-dimensional example. Two further papers, Wilson's ENN and AdaBoost, are theoretical and report no datasets at all. At the other end, NBU reports 92 datasets, k-means SMOTE 90, DLexiBoost 87, RBBoost 86, G-SMOTE 69, GDO 52 and SplitBal 46. Excluding the two surveys, the median is 19.

These counts are not directly comparable, and treating them as a measure of experimental rigour is a mistake. Three mechanisms inflate them. First, binarisation multiplication: 66 of RBBoost's 86 sets are KEEL binary tasks derived from only 16 original multi-class sources, and SVDD-WSMOTE expands 16 UCI sources into 24 tasks by recombining the classes of Pageblock and Yeast. Second, programmatic undersampling: k-means SMOTE generates up to six higher-IR copies of each of its 12 UCI sets by discarding minority instances, and G-SMOTE and RSMOTE do the same at factors of two and three. Third, parameter sweeps counted as datasets: NBU's 92 datasets include 66 simulated sets that are one generator evaluated on a six-by-eleven grid of imbalance ratio against overlap. Once these are collapsed, the number of genuinely independent data sources per study is far smaller, commonly between eight and twenty.

The critical review by Hassanat et al. reaches the same conclusion from the other direction: tabulating 72 oversampling methods, it finds a median of roughly nine datasets per paper, with about two thirds using ten or fewer, and almost all restricted to binary problems.

4.2 How are datasets selected?

Across the corpus, four selection regimes are observable, and they differ sharply in how much they constrain the result.

(a) Application-driven. The earliest work selects no benchmark at all: the data is the problem. SHRINK is built on 937 dark regions extracted from nine SAR images of the Juan de Fuca strait, with 41 positives; Cluster-SMOTE constructs its own Notre Dame traffic captures because the DARPA'98 benchmark had been criticised as unrepresentative; RACOG motivates its discrete model with a smart-home prompting dataset; AMDO adds precipitation-phase records from 888 Chinese weather stations; GDO adds author-collected video-traffic and esophageal-cancer text data. This regime yields the most externally valid evidence and the least reproducible, since several of these datasets, including the RUSBoost software-project sets SP1, SP3 and CCCS12, were never released.

(b) Explicit inclusion rules. A minority of studies state a criterion that a reader could reapply. The most influential is the rule in the OVO-SMOTE binarisation study, that a dataset counts as imbalanced when a class holds under 40% of the majority class, equivalently IR above 1.5; AB-SMOTE adopts it verbatim. AMDO tightens it with a floor on absolute minority size, requiring IR above 1.5 and at least ten instances in the smallest class so that cross-validation remains meaningful. MLOS imposes a dual threshold on both imbalance and measured overlap, requiring IR above 10 and an augmented R-value above 0.3. SplitBal applies the mirror-image rule, admitting only sets with IR of roughly eight or above. KNNOR filters to datasets under 1000 instances and then draws 19 at random, which is transparent but narrow. CISO and RBO go furthest by separating a tuning suite from the evaluation suite, CISO additionally fixing datasets, baselines, classifiers and tests in a preregistered protocol.

(c) Difficulty-grounded selection. A line of multi-class work selects datasets because prior analysis has shown them to be hard, not merely skewed. MC-CCR, MC-RBO and MC-NCLWO all cite Saez et al. for the finding that their chosen KEEL sets contain small disjuncts, frequent borderline and noisy instances, and class overlap. SHSampler and GDHS quantify overlap per dataset using Fisher's discriminant ratio and report it beside the imbalance degree. GMMSampling requires two conditions jointly, that minority classes be much smaller than majority ones and that an example-level safe-level analysis confirm they are genuinely difficult. This is the most defensible regime in the corpus and the least common.

(d) Inheritance. The largest group states no criterion and reuses an existing suite. A-SUWO, MBS and SOS give no rationale beyond "publicly available datasets". QC-SMOTE adopts the 30-dataset VS-SMOTE suite together with its published baseline numbers. EMDO deliberately reuses the AMDO benchmark so that the comparison is exact. The sharpest case is a single research group: MC-CCR and MC-RBO, published in the same year by the same authors, evaluate on an identical list of 20 KEEL datasets, and MC-NCLWO uses a strict superset of that list, adding only penbased, Dry-Bean and shuttle. Three papers, one benchmark, and the Saez-based selection rationale is inherited along with the data. Inheritance makes results commensurable within a lineage and simultaneously freezes the field's blind spots: the same ecoli, glass, yeast, page-blocks and vehicle variants recur in paper after paper, so a method that happens to suit their geometry will appear to generalise when it has only been retested.

4.3 What are these datasets like?

Provenance. UCI appears in 54 of the 69 studies and KEEL in 29; the two are not independent, because most KEEL imbalanced sets are binarised UCI data. Synthetic generators appear in 22, private or application data in 15, and image benchmarks in 5. Within multi-class work the concentration is sharper still, since most papers draw on the same KEEL suite of roughly twenty sets; MKC-SMOTE is the clearest counterexample, bringing in Absenteeism at Work, HCV, Bar Crawl, Avila, Covertype, Poker Hand and Connect-4, none of which appear elsewhere in this corpus. Specialist repositories appear only at the margins: PROMISE for software defects in MLOS and GDHS, microarray collections in FW-SMOTE, ordinal-regression benchmarks in SMOM and OREM-M, and Kaggle in MBS and SOS.

Imbalance. Most benchmarks sit in a band of moderate skew. The modal range is roughly IR 1.5 to 130, bounded below by the OVO-SMOTE convention and above by abalone19, the most extreme set in the standard KEEL suite. Beyond that band, imbalance appears only where a specific paper reaches for it: Shuttle at IR 4558 in OVO-SMOTE and 5685 in EMDO, KDDCUP DOS-versus-R2L at 3449 in SPE, and MNIST at IR 5000 under the step-imbalance profile of Buda et al. The mildest benchmark in the corpus is SOS, which never exceeds IR 9.

Scale and dimensionality. The corpus is dominated by small tabular data. A typical benchmark holds between 100 and 10000 rows and fewer than 40 features. Only a few studies leave that regime: SPE evaluates on 285k to 6.4M rows and reports that SMOTE and cleaning baselines simply could not be run at that size, GDO adds ten UCI sets above 4000 rows, RSMOTE includes Online_Retail at 541909 rows, and among multi-class work only MKC-SMOTE (Poker Hand, 24811 rows) and MC-NCLWO (Dry-Bean, 13611 rows) exceed 10000. High dimensionality is rarer still. FW-SMOTE is the only study built around an n much smaller than p regime, with twelve microarray sets of 50 to 203 samples and up to 17404 features; COG-OS reaches 31472 features on TREC-5 text; DLexiBoost uses 2048-dimensional Inception features.

Difficulty factors. Class overlap is named by 30 studies, small disjuncts by 14, label noise by 11 and feature noise by 4. The strongest designs make the factor an experimental variable rather than a description: NBU crosses six imbalance ratios with eleven overlap levels, SPIDER2 varies the borderline fraction from 0 to 70% across three minority shapes, RBO injects both label and feature noise at graded levels, MC-CCR injects label noise from 0 to 25%, and MC-NCLWO injects Gaussian feature noise from 5 to 30%. MC-MBRC carries the binary overlap-by-IR grid into the multi-class setting.

Attribute types and preprocessing. A majority of methods assume purely numeric features, and several state the restriction explicitly. Where mixed types appear they are handled inconsistently: SMOTE-NC and a VDM distance in the earliest work, integer encoding in RBO, one-hot encoding in MC-NCLWO and CISO, equal-width discretisation of all continuous attributes in RACOG. Preprocessing hygiene also varies: CISO is alone in fitting standardisation and mean imputation inside each training fold, while many studies do not state whether normalisation preceded the split.

4.4 The multi-class complication

In binary problems the minority class is given. In multi-class problems it must be defined, and the corpus contains at least four incompatible definitions. SMOM, SCUT and SHSampler treat any class below the mean class size as minority. OREM-M oversamples any class whose imbalance degree exceeds 1.2, and MKC-SMOTE uses a threshold of 1.45 on the ratio of the largest class to each class. MDO and SOUP publish hand-made per-dataset lists of which classes count as minority. GMMSampling adds a difficulty test to the size comparison. The imbalance ratio itself is reported under at least three definitions: the ratio of the largest to the smallest class, the per-class ratio against the largest class, and the summed pairwise measure introduced by SMOM and reused by OREM-M and GDHS, under which values of 150 to 480 are routine and are not comparable with max-over-min figures from other papers. Any cross-paper table of multi-class results must therefore state which definition each row uses. The risk is not hypothetical: CBHSID reports Contraceptive at 5.6 and Dermatology at 36.94, where every other paper that uses those datasets reports 1.89 and 5.55, and it does not state which definition produced its figures. Nine distinct measures are in circulation across the corpus: largest over smallest class; majority over minority after binarisation; percentage minority; the per-class ratio N_max/N_i with a minority threshold (1.45 in MKC-SMOTE, an imbalance degree of 1.2 in OREM-M); the summed pairwise multi-class ratio of SMOM, reused by OREM-M, GDHS and MKC-SMOTE, under which values of 150 to 480 are routine; the imbalance degree of Ortigosa-Hernandez et al. used by SHSampler; the minimum class prior, which is all DSRBF reports; the coefficient of variation of class sizes used by COG-OS; and, in CBHSID, none that is stated. We retain all nine and label every quoted figure with the measure that produced it, rather than dropping the incompatible ones, because the spread of definitions is itself a finding about the field.

4.5 One name, several datasets

Pooling results by dataset name is the most common way a cross-paper comparison in this field goes wrong. Having recovered seven characteristics tables, which cover eight studies because MC-CCR and MC-RBO share one, we find that a name is frequently published with different contents, and we therefore record every version separately rather than reconciling them. Twenty of the seventy-five distinct names carry a genuine conflict.

Four kinds of collision occur. The first is a different source release: MC-RBO and MC-CCR evaluate on a 7200-instance thyroid while MC-NCLWO and CBHSID use a 720-instance dataset of the same name, and SOUP and RUSBoost use solar flare 2 at 1389 instances where MC-RBO and MC-NCLWO use solar flare 1 at 1066. The second is a curation difference: dermatology appears both as the UCI original with 366 instances and 34 features (doi:10.24432/C5FK5P), which SOUP uses, and as the 358-instance KEEL version used by MC-RBO and MC-NCLWO, the difference being the eight records whose age attribute is missing; cleveland appears with distributions summing to 297 and to 303. The third is class subsetting: MKC-SMOTE evaluates on ecoli with 5 of the 8 classes, yeast with 8 of 10, zoo with 5 of 7 and glass with 5 of 6, which moves ecoli's imbalance ratio from 71.50 to 4.93 and yeast's from 92.60 to 13.23. The fourth is aggregation and binarisation: SOUP merges opel and saab so vehicle has three classes rather than four, and EasyEnsemble reduces the same source to a two-class opel-versus-rest task at ratio 3.0.

None of these is an error. Each is a legitimate experimental choice, and each produces a different learning problem. What is an error is to average them. We therefore treat the variant, not the name, as the unit of record, and report the instance count, class count and class distribution alongside every imbalance ratio we quote.

A smaller class of discrepancy is a published figure that does not follow from the authors' own table. Two cases are worth naming because they are checkable. MC-CCR and MC-RBO both print an imbalance ratio of 1.17 for vehicle; we could not reproduce that value from the distribution 199/212/217/218 under any of the nine measures catalogued below, and largest-over-smallest gives 1.10, which is what MC-NCLWO and SHSampler report. Since the two papers share one table, the figure propagated with it. MKC-SMOTE prints 18.91 for car evaluation where its own distribution 1210/384/69/65 gives 18.62; the value 1210/64 gives 18.91 exactly, which suggests the smallest class was keyed as 64. We keep the published figures, mark them, and exclude them from pooled statistics rather than silently correcting them.

We also correct four cells against their sources, noting the correction in each case rather than overwriting the record: MC-RBO's automobile is 159 instances, not the printed 150, since its own distribution sums to 159 and MC-NCLWO reports 159; its page-blocks is 5473 per the UCI record (doi:10.24432/C5J590), the missing instance tracing to a class printed as 87 where MC-NCLWO prints 88; its ecoli distribution 143/77/2/235/20/5/52 is a run-together of 2 and 35 and reads 143/77/2/2/35/20/5/52, summing to 336 and giving the stated ratio of 71.50; and, outside MC-RBO, the canonical dermatology record is the UCI original of 366 instances (doi:10.24432/C5FK5P), with the 358-instance KEEL version kept as its own variant rather than overwritten. Separately, MC-RBO's hayes-roth row prints four values for three classes, the first repeating the instance count; this is flagged, not corrected.

4.6 Where the imbalance comes from

A property that is rarely stated and strongly affects interpretation is whether the skew is natural. Sixty-three studies use at least one naturally skewed dataset, but 32 also binarise a multi-class source, 19 generate synthetic data, 7 manufacture higher ratios by discarding minority instances, and 3 impose a parametric imbalance profile. The last group is the most explicit: Buda et al. define step and linear imbalance with parameters for the fraction of minority classes and the majority-to-minority ratio, and GAMO constructs monotonically descending per-class counts for every image benchmark it uses. Manufactured imbalance buys control over the independent variable at the cost of the small disjuncts, overlap and label noise that make real imbalanced data hard, which is precisely the distinction the difficulty-grounded selection regime in Section 4.2(c) is designed to preserve.

4.7 A taxonomy for the datasets

We organise the benchmarks along six orthogonal axes, so that any dataset in the corpus receives one value on each. D1 Provenance separates curated repositories (UCI, KEEL, PROMISE, ordinal-regression collections), application-native data, controlled synthetic generators, and perceptual benchmarks (image and text). D2 Imbalance severity bands the ratio as marginal (under 3), moderate (3 to 9), high (9 to 30), severe (30 to 100) and extreme (above 100). D3 Class structure separates natively binary data, binarised multi-class data, and natively multi-class data, the last subdivided into multi-minority, multi-majority and gradual configurations. D4 Scale and dimensionality crosses sample size (micro under 500, small 500 to 5k, medium 5k to 50k, large above 50k) with feature count (low under 20, mid 20 to 100, high above 100, with n much less than p as a separate case). D5 Difficulty profile records which factors the data carries: class overlap, small disjuncts, label noise, feature noise, absolute rarity, mixed attribute types and missing values. D6 Imbalance origin records natural skew, binarisation, class merging or re-cutting, minority undersampling, synthetic generation, and parametric imbalance profiles.

Reported against these axes, the corpus occupies a narrow region: curated provenance, moderate to high severity, binary or binarised structure, small scale and low dimensionality, with difficulty asserted rather than measured and origin frequently unreported. The sparsely populated cells, large-scale data, genuinely high-dimensional data, naturally multi-class data with measured difficulty, and mixed-type attributes, are where the next generation of benchmarks is most needed.

4.8 Threats to validity of the evidence base

  • Benchmark concentration. A small set of dataset families, ecoli, glass, yeast, page-blocks, vehicle, pima and abalone, carries a large share of all reported results, and their KEEL variants are derived from the same underlying sources.
  • Count inflation. Dataset counts are driven by resampling policy as much as by breadth of evidence, so they should not be read as a quality signal.
  • Unstated inclusion rules. Most studies do not state why their datasets were chosen, which leaves selective reporting undetectable.
  • Irreproducible components. Several studies report on proprietary data that was never released.
  • Definitional drift in multi-class work. Incompatible minority definitions and IR formulas make many multi-class comparisons unsound as published.
  • Shared benchmarks within a lineage. MC-CCR and MC-RBO use an identical 20-dataset list and MC-NCLWO a superset of it, so three of the most cited recent multi-class results rest on one benchmark and corroborate each other only in appearance.
  • Name collisions. Twenty of seventy-five dataset names in the recovered tables denote more than one dataset, differing in release, curation, class subset or binarisation. Any meta-analysis that keys on the name alone will average incompatible problems.
  • Unreproducible published ratios. At least two imbalance ratios in the corpus do not follow from the authors' own class distributions, and one of them has propagated across two papers through a shared table.
  • Table opacity. In several papers the dataset characteristics table is embedded as an image, which blocks automated extraction and makes independent re-tabulation of the field slow and error prone.
  • Validation by downstream metric only. As Hassanat et al. argue, the field judges synthetic samples solely by classification scores and almost never tests whether the generated points are plausible members of the minority class.
