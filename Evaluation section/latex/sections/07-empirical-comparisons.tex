\section{Empirical Comparisons and Benchmarks}
\label{sec:empirical}

Every method in the SMOTE family is judged in the same way. Its authors resample a set of benchmark datasets, train one or more classifiers on the result, score the classifiers on held-out data with a few measures, compare the scores with those of competing methods, and, in most recent papers, test whether the differences are larger than chance. The method itself is never measured; only its effect on a downstream classifier is. Each choice in this chain, from the datasets to the statistical test, can change which method comes out ahead, and the choices have changed a great deal since SMOTE was introduced in 2002~\parencite{chawla2002smote}.

This section is written for a reader who is about to propose or compare a SMOTE-family method and wants to know how such a study is evaluated today, why it is evaluated that way, and what a reviewer can reasonably expect. We read the evaluation of all 71 empirical studies in our corpus and follow the chain in order. After the corpus and the way we read it (Section~\ref{sec:empirical-corpus}) and a comparison of SMOTE's own evaluation with today's (Section~\ref{sec:empirical-thennow}), we take each link in turn: the benchmark data and the task (Section~\ref{sec:empirical-data}), the base learners (Section~\ref{sec:empirical-learners}), the competitors and baselines (Section~\ref{sec:empirical-baselines}), the performance measures, including why G-mean became the most reported one and how it differs from F1 (Section~\ref{sec:empirical-measures-in-use}), evidence that does not come from a classifier (Section~\ref{sec:empirical-direct}), the validation protocol and the choice of settings (Section~\ref{sec:empirical-protocol}), statistical testing (Section~\ref{sec:empirical-tests}) and reporting (Section~\ref{sec:empirical-reporting}). We then ask why evaluation practice changed when it did (Section~\ref{sec:empirical-why}) and what the comparisons conclude (Section~\ref{sec:empirical-outcomes}), collect the recommendations into a protocol for a new study (Section~\ref{sec:empirical-protocol-new}), and state the limits of our analysis (Section~\ref{sec:empirical-threats}). Appendix~\ref{sec:empirical-appendix} describes the illustrative runs we use in two places and gives the coding of every study.

The short answers are these. SMOTE was evaluated with three learners and three quantities (AUC, the true positive rate and the true negative rate). A typical study published since 2021 still uses three learner families and three measures, but studies no longer agree on which: the 24 recent studies draw on 12 learner families and 21 measures and use 19 different combinations of measures. Most of the change in learners follows a change of toolkit, from Weka and KEEL to Python and scikit-learn, and most of the change in measures follows the absorption of single-class rates into composite measures, the growth of benchmarks and the growth of multi-class work. G-mean is now the most reported measure because it measures exactly what resampling changes, the balance between the true positive and true negative rates, and for the same reason it can make any shift of the decision threshold look like an improvement. Every study evaluates through classification, direct checks of the resampled data are rare (6 studies), simple non-resampling baselines such as class weighting are almost never included, and the weakest step of the typical evaluation is the choice of settings, which in half of the studies is made on the data used to report results.

\subsection{Corpus, Coding and How to Read the Numbers}
\label{sec:empirical-corpus}

The corpus is the SMOTE25 collection selected in Section~\ref{sec:methodology}: 76 papers after duplicate copies were merged. Of these, 71 report an empirical evaluation on imbalanced data; the other five are two surveys~\parencite{pradipta2021smote,hassanat2022stop} and three foundational works that predate imbalance-aware evaluation~\parencite{wilson1972asymptotic,tomek1976modifications,freund1997adaboost}. Among the 71 empirical studies, 39 evaluate binary problems, 19 multi-class problems and 13 both (Figure~\ref{fig:empirical-corpus}). Thirty-seven of the 71 are SMOTE-family methods in the sense of Section~\ref{sec:taxonomy} (34 core and 3 partial members: 24 binary only, 5 multi-class only and 8 for both settings); the others are competing resamplers, ensembles, cost-sensitive methods, generative models and empirical studies that SMOTE-family papers compare with. We report results for all 71 and point out where the 37 family members behave differently.

To show change over time we group the studies into four periods of similar size: 1997 to 2009 (14 studies), 2010 to 2015 (13), 2016 to 2020 (20) and 2021 to 2026 (24). A statement such as ``G-mean rose from 29\% to 71\%'' means that 4 of the 14 studies of the first period and 17 of the 24 studies of the last period report G-mean. With periods this small, one study moves a share by 4 to 8 points, so we only call a change a trend when it exceeds 15 points or when a practice appears or disappears completely.

For each study we coded, from the full text and supplementary files, the benchmark data, the base learners, the competing methods, every measure together with its exact definition, the validation protocol, how hyperparameters were chosen, the statistical tests and the form in which results are reported. A field the paper does not state was coded as not reported, never inferred. A second reading recorded why each study made its choices: the reasons it gives for its data, learners and measures, the measure and the data used to choose its settings, the methodological sources it cites, and any evidence about the resampled data that does not come from a classifier. Every code from the second reading carries a page reference and a short verbatim quote, so it can be checked against the paper \TODO{a co-author to verify the second-reading quotes before submission}. We also recorded which software a study names (for example Weka, KEEL, MATLAB, scikit-learn or imbalanced-learn). Only named software is counted, so these counts are lower bounds. Table~\ref{tab:empirical-studies} in the appendix lists the main codes of every study.

When we explain why a practice changed, we use three levels of evidence and say which one applies. Direct evidence means that the studies themselves give the reason, or that the link can be measured in the corpus, for example the share of composite-measure users who still report recall separately. Circumstantial evidence means that timing and association point the same way but no study states the link, for example the parallel rise of Python and of CART. A conjecture is a mechanism we propose that the corpus cannot confirm. None of these is a causal estimate in the statistical sense; with 71 studies and no intervention, the corpus can show that a change and its proposed cause move together and that the studies say so, not more.

To see whether careful evaluation is rewarded, we took citation counts from OpenAlex on 18 October 2026, available for 70 of the 71 studies, and compare the median number of citations of the studies that follow a practice with that of the studies that do not (Section~\ref{sec:empirical-why}). We report the raw counts. A count has to be read against the year of publication, because older papers have had more time to be cited: the median study published up to 2009 has 1,738 citations, against 21 for the studies published since 2021. Every comparison therefore gives the median year of each group next to its median count, and where the two groups differ in age we also compare studies of the same period.

\paragraph{Relation to the fifteen-year review.} This analysis complements the review that marked the fifteenth anniversary of SMOTE~\parencite{fernandez2018smote}. That review catalogued more than 85 extensions by seven properties of their design (how the instances to oversample are selected, integration with undersampling, the type of interpolation, operation under changes of dimensionality, adaptive generation, relabelling and the filtering of noisy synthetic instances), listed SMOTE-based ensembles and 22 SMOTE-based methods for 12 other learning paradigms, and set out the open challenges: small disjuncts, noise, class overlap, dataset shift, dimensionality, real-time processing and big data. On evaluation it says three things. It recalls why accuracy failed on the data that motivated SMOTE, where a decision tree reached about 97\% accuracy on the mammography data while always predicting the majority class gave 97.68\%, and the two kinds of error had different costs, so the trade-off between true and false positives had to be shown with ROC analysis. It summarises the empirical comparisons available in 2018. And it recommends a validation scheme that is robust to the dataset shift induced by partitioning. It does not examine which measures, protocols or tests the studies use, which is what this section adds. Table~\ref{tab:empirical-review15} lists the points of the review that bear on evaluation and what the studies published since did; we return to each of them below (Sections~\ref{sec:empirical-baselines}, \ref{sec:empirical-direct}, \ref{sec:empirical-protocol} and~\ref{sec:empirical-why}).

\subsection{From SMOTE (2002) to Today: Learners and Measures}
\label{sec:empirical-thennow}

\paragraph{What SMOTE measured.} The original paper evaluated SMOTE on nine datasets with three learners, the C4.5 decision tree, the Ripper rule learner and naive Bayes, chosen so that SMOTE could be compared with methods that handle misclassification costs directly~\parencite{chawla2002smote}. Each dataset was split by a single 10-fold cross-validation. For every amount of oversampling the paper recorded two rates, the percentage of minority examples classified correctly (the true positive rate, TPR) and the percentage of majority examples classified incorrectly (the false positive rate, one minus the true negative rate, TNR), drew the resulting points as ROC curves, and summarised each curve by the area under it (AUC). It also used the ROC convex hull to find potentially optimal classifiers and, for one dataset, reported the size of the pruned tree. The paper argued explicitly that accuracy cannot be used under imbalance, and it did not use G-mean, F1 or a significance test. In short, SMOTE was evaluated with three learners and three quantities (AUC, TPR and TNR) plus ROC plots, because what mattered in the problem that led to it was the trade-off between true and false positives~\parencite{fernandez2018smote}.

\paragraph{What studies use now.} Table~\ref{tab:empirical-thennow} sets this against the 24 studies published from 2021 to 2026. Two numbers have hardly moved. The median study uses 3 learner families (range 1 to 6), and 9 of the 24 studies use a single family; it also reports a median of three scalar measures. What has changed is how much the studies differ from each other. Together they use 12 learner families and 21 different measures, and among the 24 studies there are 19 different combinations of measures; only three combinations occur more than once. The 12 families are written in the papers under 32 different names, because the same algorithm is named with its kernel, library or settings (``SVM'', ``SVM (RBF)'', ``Linear SVM (LIBSVM)''); Table~\ref{tab:empirical-families} lists the algorithms inside each family.

\input{tables/empirical-families}

The most used family since 2021 is the support vector machine (13 of the 24 studies, 54\%), followed by the CART decision tree (12, 50\%) and k-nearest neighbours (9, 38\%). The SVM leads for two reasons that the corpus shows directly. It is the one strong learner that both generations of toolkits offer, as SMO and LIBSVM in Weka and as SVC in scikit-learn, so it survived the change of software described below. And it has become the default choice of studies that test with a single learner: 4 of the 9 single-learner studies since 2021 use an SVM, against 3 that use C4.5, the single learner of earlier work; one of them calls the SVM the most well-known and straightforward classification algorithm~\parencite{palli2022cbhsid}. CART is second because it is the decision tree of scikit-learn, and studies that move to Python take its tree with it.

A reader who wants to compare the numbers of two recent papers will usually find that they do not share a measure, a learner or both. The variety itself is not a problem, because different applications need different measures, but it means that the evidence for a method cannot be pooled across papers. This is the first thing a new study should plan for: if its results are to be compared with earlier work, it has to report the measures and learners that earlier work used, in addition to the ones it prefers.

\paragraph{What survived from 2002.} Of the three learners SMOTE used, C4.5 (5 of the 24 studies since 2021) and naive Bayes (5) are still in use, and Ripper is not. Of its three quantities, AUC is still reported by more than half of the recent studies (13 of 24) and TPR by 5, while TNR is no longer reported on its own and no recent study draws ROC curves. TNR has not disappeared from the evaluations, though; it is inside G-mean. The additions are G-mean, F1 and its multi-class variants, CART, support vector machines, logistic regression, random forests and gradient boosting, and a family of multi-class measures (MAUC, MG, AvAcc, CBA, CEN, macro-F1). The 37 SMOTE-family studies follow the same pattern, with a split by setting: the 24 binary-only methods report AUC most often (18 of 24), then G-mean (14), recall (12) and F1 (12), while the methods for multi-class problems report G-mean or MG first (4 of the 5 multi-class-only methods and 5 of the 8 that cover both settings), with MAUC and AvAcc next.

\paragraph{Why the learners changed.} Table~\ref{tab:empirical-learner-causes} lists every learner family with its share in each period and the cause of its rise or fall. One cause explains most of the table: the field changed its software. Before 2016, studies that name their software name Weka (one study before 2010 and six from 2010 to 2015) or MATLAB; no study names Python. From 2016 the order reverses: 6 studies name Python or a Python library such as scikit-learn or imbalanced-learn in 2016 to 2020 and 12 since 2021, while Weka is named by 4 studies in 2016 to 2020 and by only one since 2021 (Figure~\ref{fig:empirical-toolchain}). The learners followed the software. C4.5 is the default tree of Weka (J48) and of KEEL, while the decision tree of scikit-learn is CART, and scikit-learn has no C4.5 and no rule learner. Rule learners, such as Ripper and PART, output an ordered list of if--then rules: Ripper grows and prunes one rule at a time, and PART reads rules off partial C4.5 trees. Accordingly C4.5 fell from 64\% of the studies before 2010 to 21\% since 2021, CART rose from 7\% to 50\%, and rule learners fell from 29\% to none; the last study to use one is SOUP (2019)~\parencite{janicka2019soup}. The users of the two trees show the same split: 11 of the 33 C4.5 studies name Weka and 2 name Python, while 10 of the 17 CART studies name Python and only one names Weka (Table~\ref{tab:empirical-learner-use} in Section~\ref{sec:empirical-learners}). Code release, which also became common with Python, separates them as well: 13 of the 17 CART studies release code, against 8 of the 33 C4.5 studies. The tree did not disappear; it changed with the toolkit, and so did the algorithm inside it. A consequence for a reader is that C4.5 results in older papers and CART results in newer ones are not interchangeable, because the two trees split and prune differently.

The other learners fit the same pattern. Learners that exist in both toolkits survived the change: k-nearest neighbours (k-NN), support vector machines and naive Bayes. Logistic regression arrived with Python (6 of its 13 users name Python and none names Weka) and is chosen, in the words of one study, because a learner with few hyperparameters is less likely to bias the comparison through its configuration~\parencite{douzas2018kmeans}. Random forests and gradient boosting rose from 7\% to 33\%. Here the reason the studies give is realism: these are the learners practitioners use, and a resampler that only helps weak learners is of limited value~\parencite{vuttipittayamongkol2018obu,vuttipittayamongkol2020nbu,yadav2025ciso}. AdaBoost-type boosting, which represented ensembles from 2016 to 2020, has since been replaced by gradient boosting. Two families moved for reasons unrelated to software. The multilayer perceptron is carried by one line of work: all four studies of the MC-CCR line use it~\parencite{koziarski2020mcccr,krawczyk2020mcrbo,liu2023nromm,ma2024mcmbrc}, and one of them states that it adopts the learners, folds and measures of the first two. Convolutional networks appear only in the two image studies of 2018 and 2019~\parencite{buda2018systematic,mullick2019gamo} and vanish with image data, not because they were abandoned.

\paragraph{Why the measures changed.} Table~\ref{tab:empirical-measure-causes} does the same for every measure. Four causes account for nearly all of it. The first is absorption: single-class rates were not abandoned but folded into composite measures that contain them. G-mean contains TPR and TNR, and F1 contains recall and precision. As the composites spread, studies stopped reporting their parts separately. Among the studies that report G-mean or F1, the share that also reports recall on its own fell from 8 of 9 before 2010 to 5 of 21 since 2021; among the F1 users, the share that also reports precision fell from 5 of 7 to 2 of 10; and among the G-mean users, the share that also reports TNR fell from 2 of 4 to none of 17 (Figure~\ref{fig:empirical-absorption}). This is why recall fell from 71\% to 21\% while G-mean rose from 29\% to 71\%: the same quantity moved inside another one.

The second cause is scale. ROC curves and per-dataset confusion matrices can be read for a handful of datasets, not for dozens. The median benchmark grew from 6.5 datasets per study before 2010 to 23 since 2021, and the studies that drew ROC curves used a median of 12 datasets, against 34 for studies that report AUC without curves. The curves gave way to the area under them, and the last study to draw them is an image study with three datasets~\parencite{buda2018systematic}. Larger benchmarks and heavier methods are also why running time is reported more often (29\% since 2021, against 14\% before).

The third cause is a change of problem. Multi-class studies grew from 3 in the first period to 15 in the last, and binary measures do not apply to them directly. MAUC, the multi-class extension of AUC by Hand and Till~\parencite{hand2001simple}, is reported only by multi-class studies, and nine studies cite Hand and Till when they justify their measures. Macro-F1, the class-averaged F1 that scikit-learn offers as a standard option, follows the same path. Generative studies, which entered the corpus in 2019, brought their own tradition, in which a classifier is trained on synthetic data and tested on real data and scored by accuracy and F1~\parencite{xu2019ctgan,kim2022sos,ren2025goio,ren2026dgot}; four of the five studies that still report accuracy since 2016 are generative studies.

The fourth cause is the line of work a study belongs to. CBA and CEN entered this literature with MC-CCR and MC-RBO in 2020~\parencite{koziarski2020mcccr,krawczyk2020mcrbo}, and every later study that reports them is from the same line and uses the same KEEL multi-class benchmarks. AvAcc came from the KEEL group in 2013~\parencite{fernandez2013analysing} and travelled with them. Measures of this kind spread because a study that wants to be compared with its predecessors reports what they reported.

Two measures moved for other reasons. MCC appeared in 2020 after critiques of F1, which ignores true negatives, and of accuracy~\parencite{boughorbel2017optimal}; all four of its users name Python and release code, where MCC is a standard function. PR-AUC, by contrast, did not grow even though its best-known recommendation~\parencite{saito2015precision} has more than 5,000 citations. Its share stayed at 8 to 10\%. We can only conjecture why: PR-AUC needs well-ranked scores, which C4.5 and rule learners do not produce; it depends on the class prior, so averages over benchmarks with very different imbalance ratios are hard to read; and no benchmark or toolkit in this literature reports it by default. The last explanation is consistent with Section~\ref{sec:empirical-why}, where every practice that did spread had a tool or a benchmark behind it. Table~\ref{tab:empirical-drivers} in that section summarises the evidence for each of these drivers by period.

\paragraph{What this means for a new study.} The learners and measures of a study are mostly inherited from its software and from the papers it wants to be compared with. That is not wrong, but it should be a decision rather than a default. A new study should (i) state its software and versions, because the same name can hide different algorithms; (ii) include at least one learner that is sensitive to imbalance, at least one strong ensemble such as a random forest or gradient boosting, and the learners of the papers it compares with; and (iii) report the composite and ranking measures of Section~\ref{sec:empirical-measures-in-use} together with the measures of the papers it wants to be compared with, even if it argues that another measure is more appropriate.

\subsection{Benchmark Data and Tasks}
\label{sec:empirical-data}

The data decide what a comparison can show: a claim about severe imbalance needs datasets with severe imbalance, a claim of generality needs datasets that differ, and a comparison with earlier work needs the same datasets. This subsection describes the data the 71 studies use at the level of the evaluation, that is, where the data come from, how many datasets a study uses and why; the analysis of individual datasets and their difficulty is the subject of the Data section of this survey \TODO{reference to the Data section once its label is fixed}.

\paragraph{Where the data come from.} Two repositories supply most of the data (Table~\ref{tab:empirical-data}). Counted so that every study falls in exactly one group, 33 studies use UCI data but not KEEL data, 13 use KEEL data but not UCI data, 20 use both, and 5 use neither; in total 53 studies use UCI data and 33 use KEEL data. The five that use neither rely on private data~\parencite{kubat1998shrink,cieslak2006cluster}, the UCR time-series archive~\parencite{cao2013inos}, public fraud and record-linkage data~\parencite{liu2020spe} or image benchmarks~\parencite{buda2018systematic}. The two repositories have different histories. UCI is used in every period by 69 to 86\% of the studies. KEEL data first appear in 2013 and are then taken up quickly, by 38\% of the studies of 2010 to 2015, 60\% of those of 2016 to 2020 and 67\% of those published since 2021. KEEL offers ready-made imbalanced versions of UCI datasets, named after the classes they oppose (for example ecoli-0-1\_vs\_5) and already split into five folds~\parencite{alcalafdez2011keel}, which is why the same dataset can be counted as UCI in one study and as KEEL in another, and why 20 studies use both. Synthetic data appear in 30 studies, often as small two-dimensional examples next to the real benchmarks, and private or application data in 11, mostly in the early periods (36\% before 2010, 8\% since 2021).

\paragraph{How many datasets.} A study uses a median of 20 datasets (interquartile range 11 to 34, range 1 to 92), or 18 if only real datasets are counted (range 1 to 86). Benchmarks grew with KEEL: the median number of real datasets rose from 6 before 2010 to 14, 20 and 21.5 in the later periods, and the share of studies with fewer than ten real datasets fell from 64\% to 4\%. The same datasets recur. Ecoli is used in some form by 38 studies, yeast by 37, glass by 34, page-blocks by 30, new-thyroid and vehicle by 29 each and abalone by 25 (Table~\ref{tab:empirical-datafam}); most of these studies take them from KEEL (27 of the 38 ecoli studies, for example), and their use grew after 2010, when KEEL made imbalanced versions of them available. \paragraph{Can the data be reproduced?} In 53 studies all data are public, in 11 some are not, and 7 do not say. \subsubsection{Tasks Other than Classification}
\label{sec:empirical-tasks}

Every one of the 71 studies evaluates resampling through a classification task. The studies differ in the number of classes (39 binary, 19 multi-class, 13 both) and in the type of data (65 tabular, 3 mixed, 2 image and 1 time series), but not in the task. Table~\ref{tab:empirical-tasks} lists the studies whose data or output is not a plain table, and shows that even they come back to a classifier.

\input{tables/empirical-tasks}

Two studies step partly outside classification, and it is worth seeing exactly how. CTGAN is a generator of synthetic tables rather than a resampler~\parencite{xu2019ctgan}. Its main evaluation, which it calls machine learning efficacy, fits the generator on the training split of each real dataset, draws a synthetic table of the same size, trains several models on the synthetic table (a decision tree, a linear SVM, a multilayer perceptron, AdaBoost and logistic regression, and linear regression for the regression set) and scores them on the real test split, by F1 for binary labels, macro-F1 for several classes, accuracy for the MNIST-based sets and $R^2$ for its single regression dataset; the result is compared with the same models trained on the real training data. Its second evaluation needs no learner: on seven simulated datasets whose generating model is known (Gaussian mixtures and Bayesian networks), it computes the likelihood of the synthetic rows under the true model ($L_{syn}$) and the likelihood of real test rows under a model refitted on the synthetic rows ($L_{test}$), which falls when the generator covers only part of the distribution. GAMO generates minority-class images~\parencite{mullick2019gamo}. It trains a classification network on an imbalanced training set enlarged with the generated images and scores it on a balanced test set by average class accuracy and G-mean; separately, it computes the Fréchet inception distance between real and generated images of each class to compare the quality of its images with those of a conditional GAN. In both cases the evaluation that does not use a classifier either needs a known generating model (CTGAN's likelihoods) or measures image quality rather than the usefulness of the data (GAMO's distance). No study in the corpus evaluates regression as its main task, or clustering, anomaly detection, multi-label learning or data streams.

Why is everything classification? There are three reasons, and they explain also why direct measurement of the resampled data is rare (Section~\ref{sec:empirical-direct}). First, the method is defined by class labels. SMOTE creates a new example between a minority example and one of its nearest neighbours of the same class, so it needs to know which examples form the minority class, and the problem it was designed for is a classifier that neglects that class. Success is therefore defined as a better classifier, and the evaluation goes back to a classifier. Second, the shared benchmarks are classification benchmarks: the imbalanced collections of KEEL and UCI are datasets with a designated minority class, and this literature has no shared benchmark for imbalanced regression, clustering or anomaly detection. Third, judging the resampled data without a classifier needs a reference that is usually missing. The true distribution of the minority class is unknown, the held-out minority examples needed to compare against are scarce by definition, and there is no agreed measure; a classifier score avoids all three problems, which is why even CTGAN and GAMO report one.

This reflects the corpus rather than a limit of the method. The fifteen-year review lists 22 SMOTE-based methods for other learning paradigms~\parencite{fernandez2018smote}: data streams, time series, semi-supervised and active learning, multi-instance and multi-label learning, regression, ordinal regression, text, image retrieval, imprecise data and high-dimensional data. These methods redefine what the minority is. SMOTER, for example, treats extreme values of a continuous target as rare cases, chosen by a user-defined threshold, and generates the target of a synthetic case as a weighted average of its two seeds~\parencite{torgo2013smote}. Each paradigm also has its own measures, such as a prequential AUC for streams with concept drift~\parencite{brzezinski2017prequential}. Only one of these methods, INOS for time series, is in our corpus, and it is evaluated as a classifier of time series~\parencite{cao2013inos}. For the reader, this sets the scope of this section: every statement about evaluation here holds for classification and should not be carried over to other paradigms without separate evidence.

\paragraph{What this means for a new study.} Choose data that test the claim: public benchmarks that cover the range of imbalance ratios, sizes and difficulty the method is meant for, the benchmarks of the closest earlier studies, and, for a claim of generality, high-dimensional data, where SMOTE has been found to have hardly any effect on most classifiers~\parencite{blagus2013smote}. Give the reason for the choice in terms of what the data test rather than their popularity, keep multi-class data multi-class if the claim is about several classes, and release every modified dataset together with its folds.

\subsection{Base Learners}
\label{sec:empirical-learners}

\paragraph{How many and which.} Almost half of the studies use one or two learner families. Across the 71 studies, 25 use one learner family, 9 use two, 12 three, 16 four and 9 five or more; the median is three. The share of single-learner studies has not fallen: it is 9 of the 24 studies since 2021. The most used families over the whole period are C4.5 (33 studies), SVM and k-NN (28 each), CART and the multilayer perceptron (17 each), naive Bayes (14) and logistic regression (13), with random forests (12) and gradient boosting (8) rising in the last two periods. Section~\ref{sec:empirical-thennow} explains how the mix changed over time; Table~\ref{tab:empirical-learner-use} shows who uses each family, which toolkit those studies name, whether they release code, use KEEL data or address several classes, and how often the family is the only learner of a study.

\input{tables/empirical-learner-use}

A single learner limits what a study can conclude. A resampler changes the class proportions of the training data, and how much that helps depends on the learner: k-NN uses the same neighbourhoods that SMOTE interpolates in, decision trees are sensitive to pruning that removes the rules for small classes, and strong ensembles already compensate for part of the imbalance. With one learner a reader cannot tell whether a method is better in general or only fits that learner. The 25 single-learner studies use C4.5 (9), SVM (5), the multilayer perceptron (3), and other families once or twice each.

\paragraph{Why these learners.} Forty-nine of the 71 studies say why they chose their learners (Table~\ref{tab:empirical-reasons}). The most frequent reason is that the learners are widely used or well known, given by 22 studies and, in 8 of them, the only reason. The studies do write this, often in so many words: logistic regression is chosen ``as it is a commonly used classification algorithm''~\parencite{liu2020mbs}, three learners are adopted as ``three of the best-known classification algorithms''~\parencite{zheng2022shsampler}, and the very first study in the corpus selected its learners ``because of their widespread use and well-known performance''~\parencite{kubat1997oss}. Other reasons are that the learners cover different learning paradigms (15 studies), that a named earlier study used them (11), that they are simple, fast or interpretable (10), and that the same learner is used for every method so that the comparison is fair (8).

Popularity is a weak reason. It explains why a reader will recognise the learner, not what the learner tests, and it would justify the same learners for any method on any data. What a reader needs to know is which property of a learner makes it a meaningful test of a resampler: whether its decision rule depends on the class proportions it sees, which is what resampling changes; whether it produces scores, which ranking measures such as ROC-AUC need; whether it shares the neighbourhood logic of SMOTE, as k-NN does, or is robust to imbalance, as random forests and boosting partly are; and whether it is the learner of the work being compared with. Only 4 studies choose a learner because it is sensitive to imbalance~\parencite{napierala2010spider2,saez2015smoteipf,yan2025gdhs,tao2025mcnclwo}, and 4 because it matches how the method works, for example a boosting method that needs an unstable base learner~\parencite{diezpastor2015randombalance}. Following an earlier study is a sound reason when the study is named, because it makes the results comparable; 11 studies name one, as when the MC-CCR line keeps the learners of its first two papers~\parencite{liu2023nromm}. A new study should give, for each learner, the property it tests or the study it allows comparison with.

\paragraph{What this means for a new study.} Use at least four learners from different families: a decision tree (CART or C4.5), k-NN, a linear or margin-based model (logistic regression or an SVM) and a strong ensemble (random forest or gradient boosting), configured identically for every resampler, and report results per learner rather than only an average over learners. Name the library and version of each, since ``decision tree'' means C4.5 in Weka and CART in scikit-learn, and say which score feeds the ranking measures (a probability or a decision function).

\subsection{Competitors and Baselines}
\label{sec:empirical-baselines}

A comparison is only as convincing as what the proposal is compared with. A study compares its proposal with a median of 7 other methods (range 2 to 30). Plain SMOTE is one of them in 50 of the 71 studies, and its share rose from 50\% of the studies before 2010 to 79\% since 2021, so SMOTE remains the standard reference. A baseline without any resampling appears in 52 studies, but here the trend runs the other way: it was included by 86\% of the studies before 2010 and by only 54\% of those published since 2021 (Table~\ref{tab:empirical-competitors}). Random oversampling is included by 27 studies and Borderline-SMOTE by 27, ADASYN by 19 and random undersampling by 18, the last falling from half of the early studies to 8\% of the recent ones. Recent studies compare with recent methods: MDO is a competitor in 38\% of the studies since 2021 and MC-CCR in the same share, almost all of them in the multi-class line of work.

\input{tables/empirical-competitors}

\paragraph{Baselines that do not resample.} The most informative baselines are those that address imbalance without creating data. A cost-sensitive learner or instance weighting is a competitor in only 5 studies~\parencite{fernandez2013analysing,yang2018amdo,yao2021emdo,xie2022gdo,yadav2025ciso}, three of them published after the fifteen-year review restated the finding that preprocessing and cost-sensitive learning are equally good ways to address imbalance~\parencite{lopez2012analysis,fernandez2018smote}. Plain class weighting of the learner appears in one study~\parencite{yadav2025ciso}. Threshold moving, which changes only the decision threshold of a trained learner, appears in three: as threshold-moving neural networks and probability estimation trees in a study of multi-class cost-sensitive learning~\parencite{zhou2010globalcs}, as a threshold-moving neural network in DSRBF~\parencite{fernandeznavarro2011dsrbf} and as thresholding by the class priors in a study of convolutional networks~\parencite{buda2018systematic}. Cost-sensitive ensembles are somewhat more common: AdaCost is a competitor of SMOTEBoost and RAMOBoost~\parencite{chawla2003smoteboost,chen2010ramoboost} and MetaCost of SplitBal~\parencite{sun2015splitbal}.

These baselines matter because of the way resampling acts. Section~\ref{sec:empirical-gmean} shows that balancing the training data mainly moves the operating point of a classifier towards the minority class, and that G-mean and other threshold measures reward that movement. Class weighting, threshold moving and random oversampling move the operating point too, at almost no cost. A gain of a new resampler is therefore evidence of something more than a shifted threshold only if it exceeds what those simple baselines achieve. The one study that includes class weighting, run under a protocol it registered in advance, found its new oversampler no better than class weighting, random oversampling or no resampling across four learners~\parencite{yadav2025ciso}.

\paragraph{What this means for a new study.} Include four simple baselines, namely no resampling, class weighting (or threshold moving) of the learner, random oversampling and plain SMOTE, and the two to four strongest recent competitors, preferring those with public code. Run every method on the same folds, with maintained implementations such as imbalanced-learn~\parencite{lemaitre2017imbalanced} or a stated version of the authors' code, and check any re-implementation against at least one result of the original paper. Do not copy numbers from other papers, whose folds, settings and sometimes definitions of the measures differ.

\subsection{Performance Measures}
\label{sec:empirical-measures-in-use}

The measure turns the predictions of a classifier into the number a paper reports, and it is the choice that most often decides which method comes out ahead. This subsection defines the measures in use, counts them, and asks which is used most and why, and how the two most used composites differ.

\paragraph{Notation.} Consider a binary problem in which the minority class is the positive class. A classifier that outputs a score $s(x)$ predicts the positive class when $s(x) \ge t$ for a threshold $t$. On a test set, TP and FN count the positive examples predicted positive and negative, FP and TN count the negative examples predicted positive and negative, $\pi = (\mathrm{TP} + \mathrm{FN}) / n$ is the share of positives and $\mathrm{IR} = (1 - \pi) / \pi$ is the imbalance ratio. The measures used in the corpus are built from four rates:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{TPR} = \frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP} + \mathrm{FN}}, \qquad
  \mathrm{TNR} = \frac{\mathrm{TN}}{\mathrm{TN} + \mathrm{FP}}, \qquad
  \mathrm{FPR} = 1 - \mathrm{TNR}, \qquad
  \mathrm{Precision} = \frac{\mathrm{TP}}{\mathrm{TP} + \mathrm{FP}}.
  \label{eq:empirical-rates}
\end{equation}
TPR is also called recall or sensitivity, and TNR specificity. TPR and TNR are computed within one class each and do not depend on $\pi$; precision mixes the two classes and does. The threshold composites combine these rates at one threshold:
\begin{equation}
  \text{G-mean} = \sqrt{\mathrm{TPR} \cdot \mathrm{TNR}}, \qquad
  F_1 = \frac{2 \cdot \mathrm{Precision} \cdot \mathrm{TPR}}{\mathrm{Precision} + \mathrm{TPR}} = \frac{2\,\mathrm{TP}}{2\,\mathrm{TP} + \mathrm{FP} + \mathrm{FN}},
  \label{eq:empirical-composites}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{BA} = \frac{\mathrm{TPR} + \mathrm{TNR}}{2},
  \label{eq:empirical-ba}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{MCC} = \frac{\mathrm{TP} \cdot \mathrm{TN} - \mathrm{FP} \cdot \mathrm{FN}}{\sqrt{(\mathrm{TP} + \mathrm{FP})(\mathrm{TP} + \mathrm{FN})(\mathrm{TN} + \mathrm{FP})(\mathrm{TN} + \mathrm{FN})}}.
  \label{eq:empirical-mcc}
\end{equation}
The general
\[
  F_\beta = \frac{(1 + \beta^2)\,\mathrm{TP}}{(1 + \beta^2)\,\mathrm{TP} + \beta^2\,\mathrm{FN} + \mathrm{FP}}
\]
weights recall $\beta$ times as much as precision. The ranking measures do not fix a threshold. ROC-AUC is the area under the curve of TPR against FPR as $t$ varies, which equals the probability that a randomly drawn positive example receives a higher score than a randomly drawn negative one~\parencite{bradley1997use}; PR-AUC is the area under the curve of precision against recall~\parencite{saito2015precision}. For $K$ classes with per-class recall $r_k$, the multi-class G-mean is $\mathrm{MG} = (\prod_{k=1}^{K} r_k)^{1/K}$, the average accuracy used in this literature is $\mathrm{AvAcc} = \frac{1}{K}\sum_k r_k$, and MAUC averages the pairwise AUC of every pair of classes~\parencite{hand2001simple}. CBA divides each diagonal entry of the confusion matrix by the larger of its row and column sums and averages over classes, and CEN measures the entropy of the misclassifications. Table~\ref{tab:empirical-measure-defs} defines every measure found in the corpus. One name needs care: in the literature on data complexity, F1 also denotes Fisher's discriminant ratio, the most used measure of class overlap~\parencite{ho2002complexity,fernandez2018smote}, so a study that reports both should name them differently.

\input{tables/empirical-measure-defs}

\paragraph{How many measures, and how they group.} Across the 71 studies we found 27 distinct scalar measures computed from the predictions of a downstream classifier. They fall into five families and one further measure (Table~\ref{tab:empirical-measures}): overall accuracy or error (1 measure, 13 studies), single-class rates (4 measures, 32 studies), threshold composites (7 measures, 56 studies), threshold-free ranking measures (4 measures, 44 studies) and multi-class aggregates (10 measures, 21 studies), plus misclassification cost (2 studies). Counting F1 and an F-measure whose $\beta$ is not stated as one measure gives 26. Every empirical study reports at least one. A study reports a median of three: 5 studies report one measure, 18 two, 19 three, 12 four, 10 five and 7 six or more, so 23 studies rely on one or two.

\input{tables/empirical-measures}

The families answer different questions, and choosing among them is the most consequential decision of an evaluation. A composite such as G-mean, balanced accuracy or MCC asks whether the classifier, at the threshold it actually uses, treats both classes well. A ranking measure such as ROC-AUC or PR-AUC asks whether the classifier orders minority examples above majority ones, whatever threshold is later chosen. Resampling acts mainly on the first question, as Section~\ref{sec:empirical-gmean} shows, so a study that reports only composites cannot tell whether a resampler improved the classifier or merely moved its threshold, and a study that reports only ranking measures may miss the effect a practitioner cares about. Reporting one measure of each kind separates the two. For multi-class problems the averaging rule is part of the measure and must be stated: MG is zero as soon as one class has zero recall, AvAcc is not, and macro-, micro- and weighted averages of F1 can order methods differently.

\paragraph{The primary measure.} Fifty-six studies name the measure that decides their main conclusion. ROC-AUC is named most often (16 studies, and one more names the ROC curve), followed by an F-measure (8), G-mean (7), AvAcc (4), recall or a sensitivity-based measure (4), PR-AUC (2) and misclassification cost (2); 10 studies, most of them multi-class, name two or more measures jointly, such as MAUC and MG. The other 15 report several measures without saying which one decides.

\paragraph{Where the measures come from.} When the studies justify or define their measures they cite a small set of sources (Table~\ref{tab:empirical-measure-sources}): Bradley's analysis of AUC~\parencite{bradley1997use} and the survey of He and Garcia (11 studies each), Hand and Till's MAUC~\parencite{hand2001simple} (9), and then work on ROC analysis and on the measures of Kubat and colleagues~\parencite{kubat1997oss}. The reasons they give are discussed with G-mean below (Table~\ref{tab:empirical-gmean-reasons}).

\input{tables/empirical-measure-sources}

\paragraph{Definitions are often wrong or missing.} In 14 of the 71 studies (20\%) at least one measure is defined in a way that changes its meaning, or its definition cannot be recovered (Table~\ref{tab:empirical-metric-issues}). Two studies call the single-threshold quantity $(1 + \mathrm{TPR} - \mathrm{FPR})/2$, which is balanced accuracy, ``AUC''~\parencite{datta2020dlexiboost,xie2022gdo}; one defines G-mean as $\sqrt{\mathrm{Recall} \cdot \mathrm{Precision}}$~\parencite{liu2020spe}; two compute MAUC from hard labels instead of scores~\parencite{zheng2022shsampler,yan2025gdhs}; two define AvAcc as the mean one-vs-rest accuracy, which is inflated by true negatives~\parencite{ma2024mcmbrc,tao2025mcnclwo}; and others leave the multi-class averaging unstated~\parencite{agrawal2015scut,palli2022cbhsid}, or, in one case, define F1 as the harmonic mean of TPR and TNR~\parencite{palli2022cbhsid}. The single-threshold ``AUC'' deserves most attention, because some tools report it under that name, so a reader comparing AUC values across papers may be comparing two different quantities. These problems also go with fewer citations. Studies with a definition problem have a median of 25 citations, against 218.5 for the others; they are more recent (median year 2022 against 2017.5), but the gap remains within each period: 105 against 292.5 citations among the studies of 2010 to 2015, 59 against 260.5 among those of 2016 to 2020, and 18 against 41 among those published since 2021.

\input{tables/empirical-metric-issues}

\paragraph{What this means for a new study.} Define every measure with a formula, state which class is positive, state the threshold (the default of the learner, a tuned threshold, or none for ranking measures) and, for multi-class problems, the averaging rule. Report at least one threshold composite and one ranking measure; for a highly imbalanced problem, add PR-AUC to ROC-AUC. Declare the primary measure before running the experiments, and give the reason from the application rather than from the results.

\subsubsection{Why G-mean Became the Most Reported Measure}
\label{sec:empirical-gmean}

G-mean is reported by 41 of the 71 studies (58\%), more than any other measure, followed by F1 or F-measure (35), ROC-AUC (33), recall (29) and precision (20). Its share rose in every period, from 29\% before 2010 to 54\%, 65\% and finally 71\% of the studies published since 2021, and it is also the most used measure in multi-class studies, where it appears as MG (11 of 19). It is the only one of the main measures that rose steadily: over the same four periods ROC-AUC moved from 36\% to 62\%, 35\% and 54\%, F1 from 43\% to 31\%, 35\% and 29\%, and recall reported on its own fell from 71\% to 38\%, 45\% and 21\%. Two qualifications matter. When a paper names a single primary measure, that measure is more often ROC-AUC (16 studies) than G-mean (7), and among the 24 binary-only SMOTE-family methods ROC-AUC is still slightly ahead (18 against 14). G-mean is therefore the measure most often reported, not the one most often trusted as the final word.

Why G-mean? The answer the studies give is mostly popularity (Table~\ref{tab:empirical-gmean-reasons}). Of the 41 G-mean users, 24 say that their measures are standard in the literature and 21 that accuracy misleads under imbalance, but these reasons apply to any measure other than accuracy, and the studies that do not use G-mean give them almost as often (16 and 18 of 30). Only about six studies give a reason specific to G-mean: that it addresses the inadequacy of accuracy for this kind of problem~\parencite{kubat1998shrink}, that it suits rule and tree learners that output labels rather than scores~\parencite{napierala2010spider2}, that it rewards a gain in sensitivity only if both classes keep good performance~\parencite{das2015racog}, that it is high only when every class is handled well~\parencite{datta2020dlexiboost}, that it balances the two class accuracies~\parencite{maldonado2022fwsmote}, and that it ensures no class is overlooked~\parencite{wang2024mkc}. Popularity, however, is a consequence that needs its own cause. The cause is the way resampling acts on a classifier, and it can be stated as a chain:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Resampling changes the class proportions of the training data, usually to 1:1.
  \item A classifier trained on balanced data predicts the minority class more often. Its main effect is to move the operating point: TPR goes up and TNR goes down. How well the classifier separates the two classes, which ROC-AUC measures, changes much less.
  \item G-mean measures precisely this trade-off. It is maximal when TPR and TNR are both high and equal, and it is zero when either class is ignored.
  \item For two classes whose scores follow normal distributions with equal variance, the threshold that maximises G-mean lies midway between the two class means, which is exactly the threshold that a classifier trained on balanced data adopts, whatever the imbalance ratio. Balanced resampling moves the classifier to the point that G-mean rewards most.
  \item A proposed resampler therefore shows its clearest gain under G-mean, and later studies report G-mean to be comparable with it.
\end{enumerate}

\input{tables/empirical-gmean-reasons}

Our own illustrative runs, described in Appendix~\ref{sec:empirical-appendix}, show the size of the effect (Figure~\ref{fig:empirical-smote-vs-none}). Over 110 KEEL datasets and five learners, SMOTE beat no resampling in 427 of the 550 dataset-learner pairs (78\%) when measured by G-mean, with a median gain of 0.064. Measured by F1 it won 58\% of the pairs (median gain 0.014), by ROC-AUC 46\% (median gain 0.000) and by PR-AUC only 32\% (median change $-0.001$). This is what the chain predicts: resampling helps the measures that depend on the threshold and does little for the measures that do not.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{empirical-smote-vs-none}
  \caption{SMOTE Against No Resampling, by Measure (Illustrative Runs)}
  \Description{Stacked horizontal bars for four measures over 550 dataset and learner pairs. SMOTE is better in 427 pairs for G-mean, 321 for F1, 255 for ROC-AUC and 175 for PR-AUC; no resampling is better in 67, 172, 205 and 286 pairs.}
  \label{fig:empirical-smote-vs-none}
\end{figure}

Three properties of G-mean then made it convenient for the way this literature evaluates, which explains why it spread further than other composites. It does not depend on the class prior, because TPR and TNR are computed within each class, so the values for a dataset with IR 1.8 and one with IR 130 can be ranked together; this matters when a study averages ranks over 30 to 100 KEEL datasets, and 23 of the 41 G-mean users use KEEL data. It needs only predicted labels, so it works with C4.5, rule learners and k-NN, which produce poor scores. And it extends to several classes without a choice of averaging rule (MG). Its lineage helped as well: the first two studies in the corpus that evaluate on imbalanced data, OSS and SHRINK~\parencite{kubat1997oss,kubat1998shrink}, used it as their main measure, and eight studies cite work by Kubat and colleagues when they explain their measures.

The same mechanism is a warning. Because G-mean rewards any movement of the operating point towards balance, it rewards methods that do nothing but move the threshold, which is why the baselines of Section~\ref{sec:empirical-baselines} matter. G-mean also ignores precision: on a dataset with a large imbalance ratio, a classifier can reach a high G-mean while most of its positive predictions are wrong. Finally, 13 studies use G-mean to choose their settings on the same data on which they report it, which inflates exactly the number the reader sees (Section~\ref{sec:empirical-protocol}).

\paragraph{What this means for a new study.} G-mean is a reasonable measure for a resampler, but it should never be the only one. Report it together with a ranking measure, include class weighting or threshold moving and random oversampling as baselines, and if the method is tuned, tune it on data that are not used to report G-mean.

\subsubsection{G-mean Versus F1}
\label{sec:empirical-f1}

G-mean and F1 are the two most reported composites and are often reported side by side, so a reader should know when they disagree. Both contain recall. They differ in the second component (Equation~\ref{eq:empirical-composites}). G-mean asks what share of each class is classified correctly and takes the geometric mean of the two shares; each share is computed within its own class, so the size of the classes does not enter. F1 combines recall with precision, the share of positive predictions that are correct, and does not use the true negatives at all. Because precision mixes the two classes, it depends on the prior:
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{Precision} = \frac{\mathrm{TPR} \cdot \pi}{\mathrm{TPR} \cdot \pi + \mathrm{FPR} \cdot (1 - \pi)}, \qquad \pi = \frac{1}{1 + \mathrm{IR}}.
  \label{eq:empirical-precision-prior}
\end{equation}
When IR is large, $\pi$ is small, and a false positive rate that is small as a percentage of the majority class becomes a large number compared with the minority class, so precision falls. TNR contains no $\pi$, so G-mean does not change. Averaging F1 over benchmarks whose imbalance ratios range from 1.8 to 130, as KEEL benchmarks do, therefore mixes the quality of a method with the difficulty implied by each dataset's IR. G-mean does not have this problem, which is one more reason it suits multi-dataset comparisons.

The two measures also prefer different thresholds. G-mean is maximised at the balanced threshold of Section~\ref{sec:empirical-gmean}. F1 penalises the false positives that a balanced classifier makes, so its best threshold lies between the balanced threshold and the threshold that maximises accuracy on the original data, and it moves towards the latter as IR grows. Balanced resampling therefore helps F1 less than G-mean, and when the classes are well separated and IR is large it can lower F1. The choice between the two measures alone can decide which method a paper declares the winner.

\paragraph{When to use which.} Use G-mean, or balanced accuracy, when errors on both classes matter, for example in diagnosis, where a missed patient and a false alarm both have a cost; when results are averaged over datasets with very different imbalance ratios; when there are several classes (as MG); and when the learner gives only labels. Use F1 when the positive class is the focus and the cost lies in positive predictions that turn out wrong, for example when each positive prediction triggers a manual check or in document retrieval, where the negative class is background that need not be reported, and when results are to be compared with work in retrieval or detection.

\paragraph{What this means for a new study.} Report both, together with a ranking measure (ROC-AUC and, under heavy imbalance, PR-AUC), because the three answer different questions and can name different winners. If one of them is to be the primary measure, declare it before looking at the results and give the reason from the problem, not from the results, and do not average F1 over datasets with very different IR without also reporting ranks.

\subsection{Evidence Beyond Classifier Scores}
\label{sec:empirical-direct}

A resampler produces data, yet almost every study judges those data only through a classifier. Only 6 of the 71 studies (8\%) examine the resampled data directly, and none of them uses such a check instead of a classifier; it is always additional evidence (Table~\ref{tab:empirical-direct}). Two-dimensional plots of synthetic samples, usually on a toy dataset, are more common (33 studies) but show a picture rather than a number, and 23 studies report nothing but classifier scores.

\input{tables/empirical-direct}

The six direct checks are of four kinds. Three studies measure how far the synthetic data are from the real distribution: CTGAN computes the likelihood of synthetic rows under a known generating model~\parencite{xu2019ctgan}, GAMO the Fréchet inception distance between real and generated images~\parencite{mullick2019gamo}, and CISO the maximum mean discrepancy and the AUC of a classifier trained to tell synthetic points from held-out minority points~\parencite{yadav2025ciso}. Two count what kind of examples the method produces or keeps, which shows where on the decision boundary resampling adds or removes points: GLOS counts safe, borderline and small-disjunct minority examples~\parencite{han2023glos}, and SPE counts majority examples by hardness~\parencite{liu2020spe}. One counts noise: SMOTE-IPF measures how many synthetic examples a noise filter removes after SMOTE, that is, how many fall in majority regions~\parencite{saez2015smoteipf}. The fourth kind certifies single points: CISO checks that each synthetic point does not enter a majority region. Outside the empirical studies, a position paper proposes a check of the same kind that needs no classifier at all: hide part of the majority class, oversample the rest and count the synthetic points whose nearest original example is a hidden majority example~\parencite{hassanat2022stop}. Five further studies inspect the trained model rather than the data, by the size of the tree~\parencite{chawla2002smote,janicka2019soup}, the number and length of the rules~\parencite{batista2004study}, the diversity of ensemble members~\parencite{diezpastor2015randombalance,zhu2023orem}.

Why are direct checks so rare? A direct check answers whether the synthetic data are good, not whether the classifier is better, and the second question is the one reviewers and users ask. There is also no common measure: the six studies use six different checks, most of which need either a known generating distribution or held-out minority examples, which are scarce by definition (Section~\ref{sec:empirical-tasks}). Yet direct checks catch failures that scores hide. A resampler that places synthetic points inside majority regions can still raise G-mean, because it moves the threshold (Section~\ref{sec:empirical-gmean}), while making the minority class less faithful. The fifteen-year review asked for exactly this kind of analysis: it named the relationship between the synthetic instances and the intrinsic characteristics of the data (small disjuncts, noise, overlap) as the main open question~\parencite{fernandez2018smote}. The response has been modest. Five of the 36 studies published from 2019 check the resampled data directly, against one of the 35 before, and only two of them count the safe, borderline or rare examples that the review's challenges refer to~\parencite{liu2020spe,han2023glos}. The six studies with a direct check have a median of 238.5 citations against 140.5 for the others, at a similar median year (2019.5 against 2018), but with six studies the comparison within periods points both ways and shows no clear pattern.

\paragraph{What this means for a new study.} Add at least one cheap direct check, computed on held-out data. Two need no assumptions about the data: the share of synthetic points whose nearest original neighbours are mostly majority examples, in the spirit of Hassanat et al.~\parencite{hassanat2022stop}, and a two-sample test (MMD or a classifier two-sample test) between synthetic points and minority examples held out from resampling, as in CISO~\parencite{yadav2025ciso}. A third relates the method to the difficulty of the data: the shares of safe, borderline, rare and outlying minority examples before and after resampling~\parencite{napierala2016types}. Report the checks per dataset next to the classifier scores, and use two-dimensional plots as illustrations only.

\subsection{Validation Protocol and the Choice of Settings}
\label{sec:empirical-protocol}

The measures of the previous subsections are only as good as the experiment that produces them. Two steps decide whether a reported difference can be trusted before any test is run: how the data are split, and how the settings of each method are chosen. Table~\ref{tab:empirical-protocol-era} shows both by period.

\paragraph{Splitting the data.} Stratified $k$-fold cross-validation is the norm (Table~\ref{tab:empirical-protocols}): 33 studies repeat it, 16 run it once and 4 use $5\times2$ cross-validation, with $k = 10$ (23 studies) and $k = 5$ (20) as the usual choices. Eight studies use a single hold-out split, 6 repeated hold-out splits, and 3 deep or generative studies the fixed splits of their benchmarks~\parencite{buda2018systematic,xu2019ctgan,mullick2019gamo}; one study does not describe its protocol~\parencite{datta2020dlexiboost}. In total 43 of the 71 studies (61\%) average over repeated partitions, a share that rose from 43\% before 2010 to 71\% since 2021; the rest report a single estimate per dataset, so their differences between methods include the noise of one particular split. The choice of $k$ changed with the data. Ten folds were the rule before 2016 (57\% and 62\% of the studies), and five folds are the rule since 2021 (54\%, against 8\% for ten). The choice of $k = 5$ is partly inherited from KEEL, which distributes its datasets already divided into five folds: 13 of the 33 studies that use KEEL data use 5-fold cross-validation, against 7 of the 38 that do not. Five folds also leave more minority examples in each test fold: a dataset with 20 minority examples leaves 4 per fold with five folds and 2 with ten.

\input{tables/empirical-protocols}

\input{tables/empirical-protocol-era}

Resampling must be fitted on the training part of each split only; otherwise synthetic points are generated from test examples and the test set is no longer independent (Figure~\ref{fig:empirical-pipeline}). Only 34 of the 71 studies (48\%) state or describe unambiguously that resampling was confined to the training data, and 36 do not say. In one study the reported numbers of synthetic samples equal the full minority-class size, which implies that the whole dataset was oversampled before 5-fold cross-validation~\parencite{almajzoub2020absmote}; another appears to normalise features before splitting~\parencite{tao2025mcnclwo}.

Stratification has a limit that matters for small minority classes. It keeps the class proportions equal across folds, but random assignment within a class can still put the examples of one region of the minority class in some folds and not in others, so that training and test folds differ in distribution; this is a form of dataset shift induced by the partition~\parencite{morenotorres2012study}. Distribution-optimally balanced stratified cross-validation (DOB-SCV) assigns neighbouring examples to different folds to prevent it, and on imbalanced data it gives a more stable estimate than standard stratified cross-validation~\parencite{lopez2014importance}. The fifteen-year review recommended it for SMOTE-based studies~\parencite{fernandez2018smote}; none of the 71 studies uses it.

\paragraph{Choosing settings.} Every resampler has settings, such as the number of neighbours $k$ or the amount of oversampling, and many learners do too. Choosing them is the weakest step in this literature (Figure~\ref{fig:empirical-tuning}). Only 9 studies (13\%) tune inside the training data, by nested cross-validation or on a disjoint set of tuning datasets~\parencite{cieslak2008ls,nekooeimehr2016asuwo,krawczyk2020mcrbo,yadav2025ciso}. Twenty (28\%) use fixed or default settings for every method, which is even-handed but can penalise methods with poor defaults. In 35 studies (49\%) the reported configuration of the proposed method, or the amount of resampling, was chosen by looking at results on the evaluation data, typically by reporting the best point of a grid run on the same folds; 25 of these say so explicitly and 10 imply it by describing preliminary runs with no separate validation data~\parencite{barua2014mwmote,abdi2016mdo,chen2021rsmote}. The remaining 7 do not say how settings were chosen. This practice has not declined: its share rose from 36\% of the studies before 2010 to 54\% since 2021, while the share that uses fixed settings for all methods fell from 57\% to 21\%.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{empirical-tuning}
  \caption{How Hyperparameters Are Chosen}
  \Description{Bars for four classes of hyperparameter selection: nested or held-out tuning 9 studies, fixed or default settings 20, selected on evaluation data 35, unclear 7.}
  \label{fig:empirical-tuning}
\end{figure}

What is chosen, and on what data? Forty-one studies choose parameters of their method, 20 hyperparameters of the classifier, 14 the best of several variants of their method and 9 the amount of resampling; 24 choose nothing. The data used for the choice are the evaluation data in 25 studies, an inner validation split of the training data in 11 and a set of tuning datasets that are excluded from the evaluation in one~\parencite{yadav2025ciso}; 19 do not say. Of the 54 studies that select a parameter value, a variant or an amount of resampling, 33 name the measure that decides: G-mean in 15, ROC-AUC in 12, F1 in 8 and AvAcc in 4 (Table~\ref{tab:empirical-reasons}). In all 33 the criterion is also one of the reported measures, so when the selection uses the evaluation folds, the proposal is tuned on exactly the number the reader sees. Recall alone decides only inside two internal rules~\parencite{das2015racog,vuttipittayamongkol2018obu}, and recall alone rewards a method that predicts everything as minority.

Choosing a model on the data that are then used to estimate its performance biases the estimate upwards~\parencite{cawley2010overfitting}, and the bias grows with the number of configurations tried, so it favours whichever method was searched most, which is usually the proposed one. Search budgets are often unequal: G-SMOTE is selected from 294 configurations against 2 for SMOTE~\parencite{douzas2019gsmote}. To see what this can do, suppose that every configuration is in truth equally good and that measured scores vary by a standard deviation of 0.02 around the true value. The best of 294 configurations then scores about 0.057 above the truth and the best of 2 about 0.011, so the method that was searched more looks better without being better. A principled alternative has existed since 2008, when a wrapper was proposed that chooses the amount of undersampling and SMOTE on the training data alone~\parencite{chawla2008automatically}.

\paragraph{What this means for a new study.} Use stratified 5-fold cross-validation repeated at least five times with fixed seeds, the same partitions for every method and learner, or DOB-SCV when the minority class has few examples, and say that resampling, scaling, encoding, imputation and feature selection were fitted on the training folds only. Then either use the settings recommended by the authors of each method for every method, which is the safer choice for a comparison, or tune every method, including SMOTE and the other baselines, by nested cross-validation with the same search budget and a selection measure declared in advance. Never report the best point of a grid evaluated on the test folds; show the effect of a parameter as a separate sensitivity analysis instead.

\subsection{Statistical Testing}
\label{sec:empirical-tests}

A significance test asks whether a difference across datasets is larger than chance would produce. Fifty-two studies (73\%) use one, and the share without any test fell from 64\% before 2010 to 17\% since 2021 (Figure~\ref{fig:empirical-tests}). The Wilcoxon signed-rank test (26 studies) and the Friedman test (25) dominate, with Holm (10), Bonferroni--Dunn (6), Shaffer (4) and Nemenyi (4) as post-hoc procedures (Table~\ref{tab:empirical-tests-era}). The $t$-test, used by 21\% of the studies before 2010 and 31\% of those of 2010 to 2015, has not been used since. Grouped by what they control, 27 studies apply a corrected multiple-comparison procedure, in 23 cases a Friedman test followed by a post-hoc test against the proposed method; 19 use only pairwise tests without correction, although they compare the proposal with a median of six competitors; 3 stop at an omnibus test; 3 use Bayesian signed-rank tests~\parencite{zheng2022shsampler,yan2025mlos,yan2025gdhs}; and 19 report no test.

Twenty-five studies cite a methodological source for their tests. The most cited is Demšar's guide to comparing classifiers over multiple datasets~\parencite{demsar2006statistical} (19 studies), followed by Friedman's original papers (6 and 2), the tutorial of García and colleagues~\parencite{garcia2010advanced} (5), and Benavoli and colleagues for Bayesian tests~\parencite{benavoli2017time} (3). The uncorrected pairwise tests matter because the chance of a false positive grows with the number of comparisons: ten comparisons at the 0.05 level without correction give at least one false positive with probability $1 - 0.95^{10} \approx 0.40$ when no method is truly better. Errors remain even among the studies that test (Table~\ref{tab:empirical-test-errors}), such as a Nemenyi diagram drawn after a non-significant Friedman test~\parencite{janicka2019soup} and a Nemenyi test without an omnibus test~\parencite{ma2024mcmbrc}.

\input{tables/empirical-test-errors}

Two limits of testing need stating. First, a significant test does not remove the selection bias of Section~\ref{sec:empirical-protocol}, because the tests are run on the same folds on which settings were chosen. Second, the unit of a test must be independent. The results of several learners on the same dataset are not independent, so ranks should be computed per learner over the datasets, or over the learner-averaged score of each dataset, rather than over pooled dataset-learner pairs, which treats five results on one dataset as five datasets and makes differences look more reliable than they are.

\paragraph{What this means for a new study.} Make the dataset the unit of every test and test each learner and measure separately. For several methods, use the Friedman test with the Iman--Davenport correction followed by Holm's procedure against the proposal (or Shaffer's for all pairs), which have more power than the Nemenyi and Bonferroni--Dunn procedures~\parencite{demsar2006statistical,garcia2008extension}; for two methods, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Report effect sizes, such as the median difference and win/tie/loss counts, next to the $p$-values, and to claim that two methods are equivalent in practice, use a Bayesian signed-rank test with a region of practical equivalence~\parencite{benavoli2017time}.

Tables dominate: 58 studies give results per dataset, 42 mark the best value in bold, 38 give average ranks and 32 a table of $p$-values. Average ranks and $p$-values grew with significance testing (from 7\% and 0\% of the studies before 2010 to 75\% and 67\% since 2021), and so did ablation studies (14\% to 50\%), parameter-sensitivity analyses (7\% to 71\%) and tables in a supplement (0\% to 42\%). Standard deviations did not: they are given by 24 studies, between 29\% and 38\% in every period.

Four gaps stand out. Thirty-eight studies compute average ranks, but only 4 draw the critical-difference diagram that shows at a glance which methods cannot be told apart. Forty-two mark the best value in bold, but only 24 give a standard deviation, so in most tables a reader cannot tell whether a bold value differs from the next one by more than the variation between runs. \paragraph{What this means for a new study.} For each measure, give one summary table with average ranks per learner, win/tie/loss counts against the main baseline and adjusted $p$-values, and one critical-difference diagram; put per-dataset means and standard deviations in a supplement; add a heat map of ranks when there are several measures and learners, the per-dataset gain against the imbalance ratio, and running time. \subsection{Why Evaluation Practice Changed}
\label{sec:empirical-why}

Section~\ref{sec:empirical-thennow} explained the change of each learner and measure. Here we step back and ask what kind of event makes evaluation practice change at all. Table~\ref{tab:empirical-drivers} lists the drivers with the evidence for each. The evidence that links a driver to a change is of three kinds: the first use of the practice comes after the driver appeared, the studies that adopt the practice cite the driver, and the studies that use the driver behave differently from those that do not, as when the users of KEEL data choose five folds more often.

\input{tables/empirical-drivers}

Practices spread when something made them cheap and expected at the same time. Significance testing is the clearest case. Demšar's guide to comparing classifiers over multiple datasets~\parencite{demsar2006statistical} gave a complete recipe, KEEL and later tutorials implemented the Friedman test with Holm and Shaffer post-hoc procedures~\parencite{garcia2008extension,garcia2010advanced,derrac2011practical}, and reviewers began to expect it. The first Friedman test in the corpus appears in 2008 and the first Wilcoxon test in 2010; the last $t$-test appears in 2015; the share of studies without any test fell from 64\% before 2010 to 17\% since 2021; and 19 studies cite Demšar for their tests. Multi-dataset benchmarks spread in the same way after the KEEL repository made dozens of pre-partitioned imbalanced datasets available~\parencite{alcalafdez2009keel,alcalafdez2011keel}: KEEL data first appear in 2013 and are used by 67\% of the studies since 2021, and studies with fewer than ten real datasets fell from 64\% to 4\%. Code release and the modern learners of Section~\ref{sec:empirical-thennow} followed scikit-learn and imbalanced-learn~\parencite{lemaitre2017imbalanced}. Other changes followed a change of problem rather than a tool: multi-class measures spread when the family moved to multi-class problems, and image data and convolutional networks came and went with a few deep-learning studies. Some changes are carried by lines of work, as with the set of multi-class measures that every study of the MC-CCR line reports. And one change followed from the mechanism of Section~\ref{sec:empirical-gmean}: G-mean is the measure under which a resampler shows its effect most clearly. In short, practice changed when there was a recipe that could be followed at once, shared data and tools that made it cheap, a new problem that required it, or a measure that showed the effect of the proposal.

Being well known is not enough. PR-AUC is recommended for imbalanced data by a paper with more than 5,000 citations~\parencite{saito2015precision} and is still used by fewer than one study in ten. Bayesian tests, recommended in 2017~\parencite{benavoli2017time}, appear in three studies from 2022 on. The equivalence of preprocessing and cost-sensitive learning~\parencite{lopez2012analysis} and DOB-SCV~\parencite{lopez2014importance} were both restated in the fifteen-year review, itself cited more than 2,000 times~\parencite{fernandez2018smote}, and neither changed the comparisons that followed: cost-sensitive competitors remain rare and DOB-SCV is never used. And the weakest step, choosing settings on evaluation data, became more common rather than less, although the bias it causes has been described since at least 2010~\parencite{cawley2010overfitting}. These practices have no tool that makes them the default and no benchmark that requires them.

\paragraph{Citations.} Nor are careful studies visibly rewarded with citations. Table~\ref{tab:empirical-cites} gives the median citation count of the studies with and without each practice, with the median year of each group; as Section~\ref{sec:empirical-corpus} explained, a difference in counts has to be read together with the difference in years. Read this way, most differences are differences of age. Studies that use KEEL data, many datasets, a significance test, a corrected test or a methodological source have fewer citations than studies without them, but they are also younger, by two to seven years. Where the two groups are of similar age, the differences are small and point both ways: studies that choose their settings on the evaluation data have a median of 129 citations against 152 (median years 2019 and 2018), and within three of the four periods they are cited more, not less; studies that report a standard deviation have 129 against 148 at the same median year. Releasing code goes with fewer citations overall (122 against 208), but the code releasers are five years younger, and within the periods the comparison points both ways. Only three practices point the same way in every period. Including SMOTE as a competitor goes with more citations (for example 238.5 against 75.5 among the studies of 2016 to 2020), a measure-definition problem with fewer (Section~\ref{sec:empirical-measures-in-use}), and so, unexpectedly, does a no-resampling baseline, although in the first two periods the group without it has only two studies, one of which is SMOTE itself. The ten most cited studies show the same picture from the other side (Table~\ref{tab:empirical-topcited}): they use a median of 8 real datasets, only 4 of them use any significance test, and 7 were published before 2010. Citations follow age and the problem a paper addressed, not the care of its evaluation. In short, the field has improved where tools and reviewers pushed it, and not where improvement would have required authors to give up an advantage.

\subsection{What the Comparisons Conclude}
\label{sec:empirical-outcomes}

An outcome is what a study concludes about its own proposal (Table~\ref{tab:empirical-outcomes}). Of the 69 studies that propose a method, 68 report it as the best overall: 27 with no stated exception and 41 with an exception they state themselves, such as a measure, learner, competitor or data condition under which the proposal is not better or not significantly better. Only one reports no advantage over simple baselines~\parencite{yadav2025ciso}, and that study is also the only one that registered its protocol in advance. When almost every study reports that its method is the best, the claim carries little information; a statement of when the method is better is more useful.

\input{tables/empirical-outcomes}

These wins do not add up to a ranking. A method that leads in one study is placed below the proposal in every later study that includes it: MDO is a competitor in 13 later studies and MC-CCR in 7, and each of those studies reports its own method ahead on its main summary measure~\parencite{naglik2024gmmsampling,yan2025gdhs,ma2024mcmbrc}. Because every paper chooses its own measures, folds and settings, and usually tunes only its own method on the evaluation data, the corpus cannot say which of its methods is better; it can only say that each is better under the protocol of the paper that proposed it.

\paragraph{A minimum standard.} To summarise how far the evaluations are from the recommendations of this section, we scored every study against seven points that are cheap to meet and that a reviewer can check from the paper: repeated partitions, a statement that resampling is confined to the training data, settings that are not chosen on the evaluation data, a corrected or Bayesian test, a no-resampling baseline, at least one composite and one ranking measure, and released code (Table~\ref{tab:empirical-standard}). The points met least often are released code (27 studies), settings not chosen on the evaluation data (29), a corrected or Bayesian test (30) and a composite together with a ranking measure (32). The median study meets 3 of the seven points, and the median rose only from 3 before 2010 to 4 since 2021. One study meets all seven, RBO~\parencite{koziarski2019rbo}; four meet six~\parencite{douzas2018kmeans,douzas2019gsmote,ren2025goio,ren2026dgot}, and 39 meet three or fewer. The score is a summary of practice, not a judgement of the quality of any single study.

\subsection{A Protocol for a New SMOTE-Family Study}
\label{sec:empirical-protocol-new}

The following protocol collects the recommendations of this section, topic by topic. Each step says what to choose and why; Table~\ref{tab:empirical-checklist} lists what the paper should then report, so that a reader can reproduce the comparison and combine it with others.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{State the question and the primary measure before running anything.} Say what kind of problem the method is for (binary or multi-class, tabular, the range of imbalance) and which measure decides success, chosen from the costs of the application (Section~\ref{sec:empirical-f1}). Why: the choice of measure alone can decide the winner.
  \item \textbf{Choose data that test the claim and can be shared.} Use the imbalanced datasets of KEEL as the core, at least 30 of them, covering imbalance ratios from about 1.5 to more than 100 and different sizes and dimensions; add four to eight controlled synthetic datasets that vary overlap, imbalance and small disjuncts one factor at a time; measure the difficulty of each dataset, for example by the shares of safe, borderline, rare and outlying minority examples~\parencite{napierala2016types}, and use it to explain where the method wins; keep multi-class data multi-class; and release the processed files and folds. Why: the dataset is the unit of the statistical test, so more datasets give more reliable conclusions~\parencite{demsar2006statistical}; the imbalance ratio alone does not describe difficulty, which depends more on overlap, small disjuncts and noise~\parencite{lopez2013insight}; and 42 of the 52 studies that modify their data do not release the result (Section~\ref{sec:empirical-data}).
  \item \textbf{Use several learners of different kinds.} At least four: a decision tree (CART or C4.5), k-NN, a linear or margin-based model (logistic regression or an SVM) and a strong ensemble (random forest or gradient boosting), configured identically for every resampler, and the learners of the papers compared with. Why: the effect of resampling depends on the learner, strong learners reduce it, and with a single learner a reader cannot tell a better method from a good fit to that learner (Section~\ref{sec:empirical-learners}).
  \item \textbf{Include the baselines a reviewer will ask for.} No resampling, class weighting or threshold moving of the learner, random oversampling and plain SMOTE, plus the two to four strongest recent competitors, all re-run on the same folds with maintained or verified implementations. Why: threshold measures reward any shift of the operating point (Section~\ref{sec:empirical-gmean}), cost-sensitive learning has been found as effective as preprocessing~\parencite{lopez2012analysis}, and copied numbers come from other folds and settings (Section~\ref{sec:empirical-baselines}).
  \item \textbf{Report measures of both kinds, and define them.} For two classes, ROC-AUC and PR-AUC computed from scores, together with G-mean (or balanced accuracy) and F1 or MCC at the default threshold; for several classes, MAUC with macro-F1 or AvAcc and MG. Give the formula, the positive class, the threshold and the averaging rule of each, and do not use accuracy or weighted F1 as the primary measure. Why: G-mean and balanced accuracy reward predicting the minority class, F1 and MCC penalise false alarms, and only the two kinds together show whether a gain comes from a moved threshold or from a better ranking (Section~\ref{sec:empirical-measures-in-use}).
  \item \textbf{Keep the test data untouched.} Stratified 5-fold cross-validation repeated at least five times with fixed seeds (or DOB-SCV when minority classes are small), the same partitions for every method, and every learned step, from resampling to feature selection, fitted on the training folds only. Why: one run gives one noisy estimate per dataset, and resampling before splitting puts information from the test data into training (Section~\ref{sec:empirical-protocol}).
  \item \textbf{Choose settings fairly.} Use the settings recommended by the authors of every method, or tune every method with the same budget by nested cross-validation and a selection measure declared in advance; never report the best point of a grid on the test folds. Why: selection on the evaluation data inflates the score of the method searched most (Section~\ref{sec:empirical-protocol}).
  \item \textbf{Test the differences correctly.} Datasets as the unit, each learner and measure separately; Friedman with Iman--Davenport and Holm against the proposal (Shaffer for all pairs), Wilcoxon for two methods, effect sizes next to $p$-values, and Bayesian signed-rank tests with a region of practical equivalence to claim that two methods are equal. Why: uncorrected pairwise tests and pooled dataset-learner pairs make chance differences look significant (Section~\ref{sec:empirical-tests}).
  \item \textbf{Check the resampled data directly.} At least one check on held-out data, such as the share of synthetic points whose neighbours are mostly majority examples, a two-sample test against held-out minority examples, or the shares of safe, borderline, rare and outlying examples before and after resampling, related to the difficulty of the data. Why: scores show that a classifier improved, not why, and not whether the synthetic points fall in the wrong place (Section~\ref{sec:empirical-direct}).
  \item \textbf{Report so that results can be read and reused.} Summary tables with ranks, win/tie/loss counts and adjusted $p$-values; critical-difference diagrams; per-dataset means and standard deviations in a supplement; gain against imbalance ratio; running time; code, seeds, folds and per-fold results (Section~\ref{sec:empirical-reporting}). Why: without standard deviations a reader cannot separate a real difference from the variation between folds, and without code a method is rarely used as a competitor.
  \item \textbf{State the scope, the exceptions and the limits.} Give conclusions per measure and learner, including where the method loses, with effect sizes and practical equivalence rather than significance alone; state the limits of the study (own implementations, chosen settings, a single source of data, what was not tested); say that the conclusions hold for classification (Section~\ref{sec:empirical-tasks}); and register the plan in advance where possible~\parencite{yadav2025ciso}. Why: when almost every study reports that its method is the best, only conditional conclusions carry information (Section~\ref{sec:empirical-outcomes}).
\end{enumerate}

\input{tables/empirical-checklist}

Our own illustrative runs (Appendix~\ref{sec:empirical-appendix}) show what this protocol asks of a real experiment. They already meet ten of its requirements: 110 KEEL datasets with their imbalance ratios, no-resampling and SMOTE baselines, five learners from different families reported separately, ROC-AUC and PR-AUC from scores together with F1 and G-mean, stratified 5-fold cross-validation on the same folds with fixed seeds, resampling and scaling fitted on the training folds only, the same fixed settings for every method, per-fold results kept, average ranks with Friedman tests and win counts, and conclusions stated per measure. They lack eleven: repeated cross-validation, class-weighting and random-oversampling baselines, a gradient-boosting learner, MCC or balanced accuracy and a declared primary measure, Friedman and Holm tests per learner over the 110 datasets throughout, effect sizes and Bayesian tests, standard deviations, running time and critical-difference diagrams in every report, direct checks of the synthetic points and of dataset difficulty, controlled synthetic data, a check of the re-implemented CISO and QC-SMOTE against the numbers of their papers, and released code, folds and results. That an experiment designed with these recommendations in mind still misses half of them is a measure of how much the protocol asks, and of why a checklist helps.

\subsection{Limitations of This Analysis}
\label{sec:empirical-threats}

Our analysis has six limits. First, the corpus is the SMOTE25 collection, chosen for its relevance to SMOTE rather than as a random sample of imbalanced-learning papers, so shares describe this corpus and not the field as a whole; it is the same list of papers, with the same 37 SMOTE-family members, that the Method and Data sections of this survey use \TODO{references to the Method and Data sections}, and statements about individual datasets are left to the Data section. Second, the periods hold 13 to 24 studies each, so a single study moves a share by 4 to 8 points, and only large changes should be read as trends. Third, the causes we give are supported by direct or circumstantial evidence or are conjectures, as stated in each case; with observational data from 71 papers none of them is a causal estimate, and counts of named software are lower bounds because many studies do not name their tools. Fourth, citation counts depend on age, venue and topic as much as on the evaluation; we report them raw, with the years of the groups compared, and they show association only. Fifth, the coding was done by one reader with a second, page-referenced pass that a second reader has not yet checked. Sixth, the illustrative runs use a single cross-validation run, re-implementations of CISO and QC-SMOTE and no class-weighting baseline; they illustrate the form of a report and the effect of resampling on different measures, not the merit of any sampler.
