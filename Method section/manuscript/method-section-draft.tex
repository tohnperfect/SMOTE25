\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\textcite{chawla2002smote} introduced the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling
Technique (SMOTE) in this journal in 2002. Instead of replicating minority
examples, SMOTE creates new ones by interpolating between a minority example
and one of its nearest minority neighbours. Sixteen years later,
\textcite{fernandez2018smote} reviewed the progress of SMOTE-based learning at
its 15-year anniversary. This survey follows the line from 2002 to the present
through a corpus of 76 papers and asks, for the methods that build on SMOTE,
what exactly they change, and in which class setting (two classes, several
classes, or both) they are shown to work.

\paragraph{What we call the SMOTE family.} SMOTE creates each synthetic minority
example by moving from a minority example $\mathbf{x}_i$ (the \emph{seed}) towards
one of its nearest minority neighbours $\mathbf{x}_j$ (the \emph{partner}) by a
random fraction of the distance between them:
\[
  \mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{new}} = \mathbf{x}_i + \lambda\,(\mathbf{x}_j-\mathbf{x}_i),
  \qquad \lambda\sim U(0,1),
\]
so that the new point lies on the segment between the two. We call a method
\emph{SMOTE-family} when it generates synthetic minority examples with this
equation, or with a rule that extends it in one of four ways: (i) a different law
for $\lambda$, for example $\lambda\le 0.5$ or a Gaussian step; (ii) a region built
around the segment instead of the segment itself; (iii) extrapolation along the
line through $\mathbf{x}_i$ and $\mathbf{x}_j$, that is, $\lambda$ outside $[0,1]$;
or (iv) a convex combination of more than two instances,
$\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{new}}=\sum_m \alpha_m\mathbf{x}_m$ with $\alpha_m\ge 0$ and
$\sum_m\alpha_m=1$. A method may change which seeds and partners it uses, how
many points it makes, what it removes afterwards, or where in the learning process
it runs, and still belong to the family. Methods that sample around a single
point without a partner (inside a sphere, on a Mahalanobis contour, along a random
direction) or draw from a fitted or learned distribution do not belong to it. The
definition concerns the generator, not the name: a method called ``SMOTE'' that
does not interpolate is excluded, and a method with another name that does is
included.

Our account rests on three elements. First, we write the SMOTE generation rule
as six decisions and record which of them each method changes
(Section~\ref{sec:smote-by-decision}). Second, we sort the methods by the class
setting in which their papers actually generate synthetic data
(Section~\ref{sec:class-setting}). Third, we give for every method an account of
how it builds on SMOTE, with references to the pages of its paper
(Section~\ref{sec:methods}). All 37 SMOTE-family papers in the corpus were read
in full for this purpose.

\section{The SMOTE Generation Rule}
\label{sec:smote-rule}

Let $S_{\min}\subset\mathbb{R}^d$ be the minority examples of a two-class
training set and $S_{\mathrm{maj}}$ the majority examples. For a seed
$\mathbf{x}_i\in S_{\min}$, let $N_k(\mathbf{x}_i)$ denote its $k$ nearest
neighbours within $S_{\min}$ under the Euclidean distance. SMOTE creates a
synthetic example by choosing a neighbour $\mathbf{x}_j\in N_k(\mathbf{x}_i)$ at
random and placing a point at a random position on the segment that joins them
\parencite[pp.~328--329]{chawla2002smote}:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{new}} = \mathbf{x}_i + \lambda\,(\mathbf{x}_j-\mathbf{x}_i),
  \qquad \lambda\sim U(0,1).
  \label{eq:smote}
\end{equation}
The amount of oversampling is a percentage $N$: at $N=200\%$ each minority
example produces two synthetic points, towards two of its $k=5$ neighbours, and
amounts below $100\%$ apply the rule to a random subset of the minority class.
In the experiments SMOTE is combined with random undersampling of the majority
class at levels from $10\%$ to $2000\%$ \parencite[pp.~331, 337]{chawla2002smote}.
The same paper extends the rule to mixed features (SMOTE-NC) and to nominal
features (SMOTE-N), the latter without evaluation
\parencite[pp.~348--351]{chawla2002smote}.

Read as a procedure, Eq.~\eqref{eq:smote} fixes six choices in the simplest way
(Table~\ref{tab:decision-points}): every minority example is a seed with equal
weight; every seed receives the same number of points; the partner is drawn
uniformly from the five nearest minority neighbours; the point is drawn uniformly
on the segment; nothing is removed afterwards; and the generator runs once,
before training. We call these the six \emph{decision points} of the rule. Writing
each of them as a symbol turns Eq.~\eqref{eq:smote} into a generation template in
which every SMOTE-family method fills the six slots in its own way:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
&\text{(1) seed selection:} && i\sim w(i),\ \ \mathbf{x}_i\in S_{\min}\\
&\text{(2) sample allocation:} && \text{repeat } g_i \text{ times for seed } \mathbf{x}_i:\\
&\text{(3) neighbour choice:} && \qquad \mathbf{x}_j\sim q(\cdot\mid\mathbf{x}_i)\\
&\text{(4) placement rule:} && \qquad \mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{new}}=\mathbf{x}_i+\lambda\,(\mathbf{x}_j-\mathbf{x}_i),\ \ \lambda\sim r(\cdot)\\
&\text{(5) post-filtering:} && S'\leftarrow F\bigl(S\cup S_{\mathrm{syn}}\bigr)\\
&\text{(6) inside a pipeline:} && \text{steps (1) to (5) run once before training, or at every step } t \text{ of a learner}
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:template}
\end{equation}
Here $w$ is the seed distribution over $S_{\min}$, $g_i$ the number of points given
to seed $\mathbf{x}_i$ (or to its cluster or class), $q$ the partner distribution
(its candidate pool, metric and weights), $r$ the law of $\lambda$ or, more
generally, the region in which the new point is placed, and $F$ a filter applied
to the enlarged training set. SMOTE is the special case with uniform $w$, constant
$g_i=N/100$, $q$ uniform over the five nearest minority neighbours, $r=U(0,1)$ on
the segment, $F$ the identity, and a single run before training.
Figure~\ref{fig:decision-points} shows the same correspondence: panel~(a) points
each decision at the symbol of Eq.~\eqref{eq:smote} it changes, and panel~(b)
shows what each decision does on two-dimensional data.

\section{Review Method}
\label{sec:review-method}

\subsection{Corpus}
\label{sec:corpus}
We call the papers analysed in this survey the \emph{SMOTE25 core corpus}. It is a
curated collection, assembled by the authors in a shared folder with four
sub-folders by problem setting (binary, multiclass, both, and surveys), and not
the result of a systematic database search. The folders hold 100 files: 12
supplementary files and 88 paper files, 12 of which are second copies of the same
paper filed under two settings. Merging the copies leaves 76 distinct papers,
published from 1972 to 2026; the number is therefore a count of what the
collection contains, not a target.

The 76 papers play four roles. Five are foundational works that later methods
build on or compare with (ENN, Tomek links, AdaBoost, the multiclass ensemble
analysis of Wang and Yao, and the CTGAN and TVAE tabular generators); 66 propose a
new method for imbalanced classification (29 oversampling, 17 hybrid sampling, 9
ensemble, 4 undersampling, 4 deep generative, 2 algorithm-level and 1
cost-sensitive); three are empirical studies of SMOTE or resampling; and two are
surveys. Measured against our definition, 37
of the papers are SMOTE-family, 3 study SMOTE without proposing a generator, 12
are related to SMOTE but generate in another way, and 24 have no direct link to
it. \claudedraft{The corpus thus places the SMOTE family among its predecessors,
its competitors and its critics, which is what the comparisons in this survey
require; it is not a census of every SMOTE variant ever published.} Each paper
was recorded in a coding sheet (the Method Map) with its approach, mechanism,
baselines, datasets, classifiers, metrics, stated limitations and code
availability.

The 15-year review of \textcite{fernandez2018smote} was assembled in the same way.
It reports no databases, search terms, time window, inclusion criteria or
screening counts; it presents the SMOTE-based approaches its authors judged most
significant (Section~3, p.~868), states that more than 85 extensions exist
(p.~871), and lists about 90 of them, from 2004 to 2017, in its Table~1
(p.~872), with further tables for SMOTE-based ensembles (Table~2) and other
learning paradigms (Table~3). Each extension is characterised by seven properties
(Section~3.1, pp.~869--871): initial selection of instances, integration with
undersampling, type of interpolation, dimensionality change, adaptive generation,
relabelling, and filtering of noisy generated instances. \claudedraft{Four of these
properties correspond to our decision points (initial selection to seed selection,
adaptive generation to sample allocation, type of interpolation to the placement
rule and partly to neighbour choice, filtering to post-filtering); integration with
undersampling, relabelling and dimensionality change act on the data around the
generator rather than on Eq.~(1), and generation inside a pipeline appears in their
review as a separate table of ensembles.}

\paragraph{How the 76 papers were chosen.} We selected the papers on the same
principle as \textcite{fernandez2018smote}: the authors collected the approaches
they judged most significant for learning from imbalanced data since SMOTE,
together with the foundational works these approaches build on or are compared
with, the empirical studies and critical reviews that evaluate them, and recent
work up to 2026. Where our survey departs from the 15-year review is in what it
then analyses. Fernández et al.\ treat every oversampling method that creates
synthetic examples by interpolation or another process as related to SMOTE
(p.~868); we fix the object of analysis by a definition. Only the 37 papers whose
generator meets the definition of the SMOTE family (Section~\ref{sec:smote-definition})
are coded and compared as methods in Sections~\ref{sec:smote-by-decision}
to~\ref{sec:method-choice}; the other 39 papers (undersampling, ensembles,
cost-sensitive learning, other generators, studies and surveys) are kept as the
context against which the family is described and are not analysed as SMOTE
methods. The systematic search of Section~\ref{sec:search} tests how far this
expert selection covers the literature.

\subsection{Systematic Search}
\label{sec:search}
To test how far the core corpus represents the SMOTE literature, and to extend it,
we defined a systematic search and ran its identification stage.

\paragraph{Protocol.} Sources: Scopus and Web of Science Core Collection (title,
abstract and author keywords), OpenAlex and arXiv for preprints, and backward and
forward citation snowballing from the 37 SMOTE-family papers. Window: January 2002
to the date of the search. Search string, applied to title, abstract and keywords:
\begin{quote}\small
(SMOTE OR oversampling OR ``over-sampling'' OR ``synthetic minority'' OR
``adaptive synthetic'' OR ``synthetic samples'' OR ``synthetic instances'') AND
(imbalanced OR imbalance OR ``minority class'' OR ``minority classes'')
\end{quote}
A record is included when it (I1) proposes a method that generates synthetic
minority examples, or changes how SMOTE is applied, for classification; (I2)
evaluates it on data; and (I3) is a peer-reviewed journal or conference paper, or
a preprint with full text, in English. It is excluded when it (E1) applies existing
resampling to a domain without proposing a method; (E2) addresses regression,
clustering or anomaly detection only; (E3) has no accessible full text; or (E4)
is an earlier version of an included paper. Two reviewers screen titles and
abstracts independently and resolve conflicts by discussion; full texts are
screened by one reviewer with a second check of a 20\% sample; every exclusion is
recorded with its reason, and the flow is reported in the PRISMA 2020 format.

\paragraph{Pilot on OpenAlex.} We ran the identification stage on OpenAlex on
29 October 2026 (articles, conference papers, chapters and preprints, 2002 to
2026) and measured, for each candidate string, how many corpus papers it
retrieves (Table~\ref{tab:search-pilot}). The final string returns 14,589
records and retrieves 36 of the 37 SMOTE-family papers; the one it misses, LS, has
none of the generation terms of the string in its OpenAlex title and abstract. Of the 75 corpus papers
with an OpenAlex record it retrieves 57; the 18 it misses are, as intended, almost
all papers that do not oversample (undersampling, ensembles, cost-sensitive and
foundational works). The pilot also exposed a limit of OpenAlex: it holds an
abstract for only 17 of the 37 SMOTE-family papers, so its ``title and abstract''
search is in practice a title search for most journal papers. The search for the
paper therefore has to be run in Scopus or Web of Science, which index abstracts
and keywords.

\subsection{What Counts as a SMOTE-Family Method}
\label{sec:smote-definition}
We call a method \emph{SMOTE-family} when it creates synthetic minority examples
by interpolating between a minority seed and a neighbour as in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:smote}, or by a rule that extends it: a different law for
$\lambda$, a region built around the segment, extrapolation along the line
through the two points, or a convex combination of several instances. The
definition concerns the generator, not the name of a method. Each paper received
one of four labels: \emph{Core} (34 papers), where interpolation is the main
generator; \emph{Partial} (3: INOS, EMDO and GLOS), where it is one of several
generators; \emph{Study} (3), which evaluates or reviews SMOTE without proposing
a generator \pcite{fernandez2013analysing}\pcite{pradipta2021smote}\pcite{hassanat2022stop};
and \emph{Not counted} (12), related to SMOTE but generating in another way or not
at all. The remaining 24 papers have no direct link to SMOTE.

Ten of the twelve \emph{Not counted} papers generate synthetic minority examples,
but not between two instances: on the Mahalanobis contour of a seed (MDO, AMDO),
inside a sphere or ball around one point (MC-CCR, SHSampler, MC-NCLWO, MLOS),
along a random direction at a Gaussian distance (GDO), by perturbing points near a
cluster centroid (CBHSID), or by hill climbing on a potential field (RBO, MC-RBO).
The other two derive from SMOTE but drop its generator: RUSBoost replaces SMOTE in
SMOTEBoost by random undersampling, and AdaBoost.NC with random oversampling
\pcite{wang2012multiclass} duplicates examples. Two inclusions are judgement calls:
G-SMOTE samples in a hypersphere whose radius is set by the chosen neighbour and
which can be shrunk back to the segment, and GAMO replaces two-point interpolation
by a learned convex combination of all instances of a class. Both are counted
because they generalise the segment rather than replace it. Nine of the ten
region- or distribution-based generators appeared in 2018 or later.

\subsection{Coding Procedure}
\label{sec:coding}
All 37 SMOTE-family papers were read in full on 28 October 2026, including
supplementary material where the main paper left the class setting open. For each
paper we recorded (i) which of the six decision points the generator changes,
(ii) a short account of how it builds on SMOTE, and (iii) its class category,
defined by what the paper shows its generator doing:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Binary only}: every experiment in which the generator creates
      synthetic data is a two-class problem. A multiclass dataset reduced to two
      classes (one class against the rest, merged classes, two classes kept and the
      rest dropped, or a ready-made split such as KEEL's \texttt{yeast-2\_vs\_4})
      counts here, and so does a multiclass extension that is described but never
      run.
\item \emph{Multiclass only}: the generator creates synthetic data with three or
      more classes kept, and is never run on a two-class problem.
\item \emph{Binary + Multiclass}: the same generator is run on at least one
      two-class problem and on at least one problem with three or more classes
      kept, without first reducing it to two classes.
\end{itemize}
A paper that generates more than one type of data (for example images and
tables) is classified for each data type separately
(Section~\ref{sec:other-data}), and its overall category combines them.
Experiments reported only in supplementary material count, since they show what
the method can do.
Every statement in the per-method accounts carries a page, section, table or
equation reference to its paper. The reading corrected the decision points of 21
of the 36 variants recorded in an earlier pass, mostly by adding decisions that
the first coding had missed.

\subsection{Seed Selection, $w$ (24 Papers)}
\label{sec:dp-seed}
SMOTE draws every minority point as a seed with equal probability. A seed rule replaces the uniform draw by a score $s$:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{x}_i \sim w(i) = \frac{s(\mathbf{x}_i)}{\sum_{k\in S_{\min}} s(\mathbf{x}_k)}, \qquad s(\mathbf{x}_i)\ge 0.
  \label{eq:dp-seed}
\end{equation}
Figure~\ref{fig:dp-seed} shows the rule on two-dimensional data.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth,page=1]{figures/smote-dp-equations}
  \caption{Seed selection: SMOTE draws seeds uniformly; a seed rule weights them by a score $s$, and a score of zero removes a point as a seed. Example: Borderline-SMOTE keeps only points with between $k/2$ and $k-1$ majority neighbours.}
  \Description{Left: the SMOTE and generalised seed rules with the Borderline-SMOTE example. Right: minority points near the majority marked as selected seeds, interior points faded, and a minority point inside the majority marked as noise.}
  \label{fig:dp-seed}
\end{figure}


The most frequently changed decision is the choice of seeds, and the papers do
it in four ways. Eight concentrate generation near the class border or on hard
examples: Borderline-SMOTE~\pcite{han2005borderline} and
AB-SMOTE~\pcite{almajzoub2020absmote} use only ``danger'' seeds,
RAMOBoost~\pcite{chen2010ramoboost} draws seeds from a ranking that favours border
points, MWMOTE~\pcite{barua2014mwmote} and A-SUWO~\pcite{nekooeimehr2016asuwo}
weight seeds by closeness to the majority class, EMDO~\pcite{yao2021emdo} and
GLOS~\pcite{han2023glos} use borderline seeds for their interpolating generator,
and Loyalty-SMOTE~\pcite{hu2026loyalty} keeps boundary samples with a Loyalty
score between 0 and $m$. Seven favour safe or dense seeds:
k-means SMOTE~\pcite{douzas2018kmeans} keeps minority-dominated clusters,
KNNOR~\pcite{islam2022knnor} the densest minority points,
MC-MBRC~\pcite{ma2024mcmbrc} safe samples by fuzzy membership,
MKC-SMOTE~\pcite{wang2024mkc} points with at least half same-class neighbours,
GDHS~\pcite{yan2025gdhs} a safe factor, and CISO~\pcite{yadav2025ciso} and
QC-SMOTE~\pcite{upman2026qcsmote} safety or trust scores. Five remove noise or
type their seeds before generation: Safe-Level-SMOTE~\pcite{bunkhumpornpat2009safelevel}
skips pairs of noisy points, CGMOS~\pcite{zhang2016cgmos} weights seeds by the
expected gain in posterior certainty, RSMOTE~\pcite{chen2021rsmote} drops noise and
separates borderline from safe seeds, SVDD-WSMOTE~\pcite{tao2022svddw} removes
overlapped seeds inside the majority boundary, and NROMM~\pcite{liu2023nromm}
splits seeds into inland, borderline and trapped. In the last four the learner
sets the seeds: SMOTEBoost~\pcite{chawla2003smoteboost} and OREMBoost sample them
from the boosting distribution, RBBoost~\pcite{diezpastor2015randombalance} may
grow the majority class when it draws inverted class proportions, and
GAMO~\pcite{mullick2019gamo} has no single seed at all.

\subsection{Sample Allocation, $g_i$ (21 Papers)}
\label{sec:dp-alloc}
SMOTE gives every seed the same number of points, $g_i=N/100$. An allocation rule shares a total $G$ out by a weight $r_i$:
\begin{equation}
  g_i = G\,\hat r_i, \qquad \hat r_i = \frac{r_i}{\sum_k r_k}.
  \label{eq:dp-alloc}
\end{equation}
Figure~\ref{fig:dp-alloc} shows the rule on two-dimensional data.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth,page=2]{figures/smote-dp-equations}
  \caption{Sample allocation: SMOTE gives each seed $N/100$ points; an allocation rule shares a total $G$ by weights $r_i$. Example: ADASYN uses the share of majority neighbours, so seeds near the majority receive more points.}
  \Description{Left: the SMOTE and generalised allocation rules with the ADASYN example. Right: three seeds that receive one, two and four synthetic points, the most for the seed nearest the majority.}
  \label{fig:dp-alloc}
\end{figure}


Ten methods give seeds unequal numbers of points: ADASYN~\pcite{he2008adasyn} by
the share of majority neighbours, RAMOBoost, MWMOTE, A-SUWO, RSMOTE, SVDD-WSMOTE,
CISO and QC-SMOTE through weighted seed draws or quotas, k-means SMOTE by the
inverse density of clusters, and NROMM by local density. Seven set quotas per
class or region: SSRBF and DSRBF~\pcite{fernandeznavarro2011dsrbf} enlarge one
class at a time, SCUT~\pcite{agrawal2015scut} and GDHS fill classes to the mean
class size, MC-MBRC to the largest class minus its overlap, OREM-M~\pcite{zhu2023orem}
draws classes in proportion to their remaining quota, GLOS uses class
discreteness and EMDO splits each class's quota across ellipsoids. LS~\pcite{cieslak2008ls}
searches the SMOTE level per data segment with a wrapper, and RBBoost draws random
class proportions. INOS~\pcite{cao2013inos} splits the quota between an
interpolating and a Gaussian generator, and in GAMO the allocation is learned.

\subsection{Neighbour Choice, $q$ (22 Papers)}
\label{sec:dp-nbr}
SMOTE draws the partner uniformly from the five nearest minority neighbours under the Euclidean distance. A partner rule changes the candidate pool $P$, the weights $\omega_{ij}$ or the metric $d$:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{x}_j \sim q(j\mid i) \;\propto\; \omega_{ij}\,\mathbf{1}\bigl[\mathbf{x}_j \in P(\mathbf{x}_i)\bigr].
  \label{eq:dp-nbr}
\end{equation}
Figure~\ref{fig:dp-nbr} shows the rule on two-dimensional data.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth,page=3]{figures/smote-dp-equations}
  \caption{Neighbour choice: SMOTE draws the partner uniformly from five nearest minority neighbours; a partner rule changes the pool $P$, the weights $\omega_{ij}$ or the distance $d$. Example: Cluster-SMOTE restricts the pool to the seed's own cluster.}
  \Description{Left: the SMOTE and generalised partner rules with Cluster-SMOTE, Borderline-SMOTE2 and FW-SMOTE as examples. Right: two minority clusters separated by majority points; the seed is joined only to partners in its own cluster, and a nearer point in the other cluster is crossed out.}
  \label{fig:dp-nbr}
\end{figure}


Nine methods restrict the partner to a region: the same cluster
(Cluster-SMOTE~\pcite{cieslak2006cluster}, MWMOTE, k-means SMOTE, NROMM), a
sub-cluster that does not cross the majority class (A-SUWO), other borderline
points (AB-SMOTE), the same ellipsoid under its Mahalanobis metric (EMDO), safe
and border samples of the same class (MC-MBRC) or the boundary set
(Loyalty-SMOTE). Five allow a majority point as partner or radius point, which the
2002 paper listed as future work \parencite[p.~348]{chawla2002smote}:
Borderline-SMOTE2, G-SMOTE~\pcite{douzas2019gsmote}, HDE~\pcite{chen2021hde},
OREM and GLOS. Four reweight the partners or directions (SMOM~\pcite{zhu2017smom},
GDHS, CISO, SVDD-WSMOTE), three replace the single partner by several instances
(KNNOR chains through all $k$ neighbours, MKC-SMOTE uses their centroid, GAMO all
instances of the class), and FW-SMOTE~\pcite{maldonado2022fwsmote} changes the
distance to a feature-weighted Minkowski metric.

\subsection{Placement Rule, $r$ (13 Papers)}
\label{sec:dp-place}
SMOTE places the point uniformly on the segment. A placement rule changes the law of $\lambda$, replaces the segment by a region $R$, or combines more than two instances:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{new}} = \mathbf{x}_i + \lambda(\mathbf{x}_j-\mathbf{x}_i),\ \lambda\sim r(\lambda); \quad\text{or}\quad \mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{new}} \in R(\mathbf{x}_i,\mathbf{x}_j); \quad\text{or}\quad \mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{new}} = \textstyle\sum_m \alpha_m \mathbf{x}_m.
  \label{eq:dp-place}
\end{equation}
Figure~\ref{fig:dp-place} shows the rule on two-dimensional data.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth,page=4]{figures/smote-dp-equations}
  \caption{Placement rule: SMOTE draws $\lambda\sim U(0,1)$ on the segment; a placement rule changes the law of $\lambda$, uses a region $R$ around the segment, extrapolates, or combines more than two instances. Examples: Borderline-SMOTE2, HDE, G-SMOTE and GAMO.}
  \Description{Left: the SMOTE and generalised placement rules with four examples. Right: a seed and a partner joined by a segment; the half nearest the seed is highlighted for lambda at most one half, dashed extensions show lambda below zero and above one, and a shaded ellipse shows a region around the segment.}
  \label{fig:dp-place}
\end{figure}


Five methods change the law of $\lambda$ or the step: Borderline-SMOTE2
($\lambda\le 0.5$ towards majority partners), Safe-Level-SMOTE (shifted towards
the safer endpoint), OREM (half the step towards majority partners), CISO (a
truncated $q$-Gaussian on a segment between two neighbours of the anchor) and HDE
(a step from $N(0,1)$, which can extrapolate). Five leave the segment: G-SMOTE (a
truncated, deformed hypersphere), MKC-SMOTE (the line from the neighbours'
centroid through the seed and beyond), KNNOR (chained steps), GLOS (a step capped
at the nearest-neighbour distance, plus two non-interpolating generators) and EMDO
(points along ellipsoid axes in pure ellipsoids). GAMO uses a learned convex
combination, QC-SMOTE keeps the best of several candidates, and NROMM uses an
optimised embedding for trapped seeds and a constrained rule for categorical
values.

\subsection{Post-Filtering, $F$ (12 Papers)}
\label{sec:dp-filt}
SMOTE keeps every synthetic point. A filter accepts candidates with a test $a$ and removes a set $R$ of examples:
\begin{equation}
  S' = \Bigl(S \cup \{\mathbf{x}\in S_{\mathrm{syn}} : a(\mathbf{x})=1\}\Bigr) \setminus R.
  \label{eq:dp-filt}
\end{equation}
Figure~\ref{fig:dp-filt} shows the rule on two-dimensional data.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth,page=5]{figures/smote-dp-equations}
  \caption{Post-filtering: SMOTE keeps every synthetic point; a filter rejects candidates with a test $a$ and removes a set $R$. Examples: KNNOR keeps a point only if all its nearest neighbours are minority; SMOTE-ENN removes examples misclassified by their three nearest neighbours.}
  \Description{Left: the SMOTE and generalised filtering rules with the KNNOR and SMOTE-ENN examples. Right: kept synthetic points among the minority, a rejected candidate among the majority, and a removed majority point.}
  \label{fig:dp-filt}
\end{figure}


Four methods clean the enlarged training set as a whole: SMOTE-Tomek and
SMOTE-ENN~\pcite{batista2004study}, SMOTE-IPF~\pcite{saez2015smoteipf}, INOS (noise
links) and Loyalty-SMOTE. Five test each candidate and reject or replace it:
KNNOR, SVDD-WSMOTE, QC-SMOTE, MKC-SMOTE (which over-generates and keeps the
candidates nearest the centroid) and OREM-M (which drops points nearest to another
minority class). Three remove or move majority examples in the regions where
synthetic points were placed: NROMM, MC-MBRC and GDHS.

\subsection{Inside a Pipeline, Step 6 (8 Papers)}
\label{sec:dp-pipe}
SMOTE generates once and the learner is trained on the result. In a pipeline the generator runs at every step $t$ of a learner $\mathcal{A}$, for example with the weights $\mathcal{D}_t$ of a boosting round:
\begin{equation}
  D_t = D \cup \mathrm{SMOTE}(D;\,\mathcal{D}_t),\quad h_t = \mathcal{A}(D_t),\quad H(\mathbf{x}) = \arg\max_y \textstyle\sum_t \alpha_t\, h_t(\mathbf{x},y).
  \label{eq:dp-pipe}
\end{equation}
Figure~\ref{fig:dp-pipe} shows the rule on two-dimensional data.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth,page=6]{figures/smote-dp-equations}
  \caption{Inside a pipeline: SMOTE generates once before training; in a pipeline generation is repeated at every step of a learner. Example: SMOTEBoost generates fresh points in each AdaBoost.M2 round and discards them after the round.}
  \Description{Left: the SMOTE and generalised pipeline rules with the SMOTEBoost example. Right: three boosting rounds, each with fresh synthetic points, feeding classifiers h1 to h3 that are combined into H.}
  \label{fig:dp-pipe}
\end{figure}


Eight methods call the generator repeatedly inside a learner: in every round of
AdaBoost.M2 (SMOTEBoost, RAMOBoost, RBBoost, OREMBoost), in every member of a
bagging ensemble (HDE), inside a wrapper that tunes the sampling levels (LS),
during the evolutionary training of a radial basis function network (DSRBF), or
inside adversarial training (GAMO).

\section{What the Method View Shows}
\label{sec:method-findings}

\emph{Changes accumulate.} The mean number of decisions changed per variant rose
from 1.7 in 2002 to 2009 (7 variants) to 2.0 in 2010 to 2017 (10) and 3.6 in 2018
to 2026 (19). Twelve of the 36 variants change four or more decisions.
\claudedraft{When a method changes the seeds, the partner, the placement and the
filter together, a comparison with SMOTE alone cannot tell which change produced
the gain.}

\emph{The partner, the placement and the filter changed most after 2018.} Since
2018, 17 of 19 variants change the neighbour choice (5 of 17 before), 11 change
the placement rule (2 of 17 before) and 9 add a filter after generation (3 of 17
before). Generation inside a pipeline did not grow (2, 3 and 3 variants in the
three periods).

\emph{The questions of 2002 have mostly been taken up, with one exception.} The
2002 paper named four directions for future work
\parencite[p.~348]{chawla2002smote}: other ways of creating synthetic points
(13 methods change the placement rule), a focus on misclassified examples (the
border-oriented seed rules and the boosting methods), and the use of majority
neighbours (five methods allow a majority partner). The fourth, an adaptive choice
of $k$, is not the subject of any method in the corpus; MKC-SMOTE still lists it as
future work.

\pnote{``Not the subject of any method'' rests on the Method Map fields; the PDF
check was not asked about $k$. Confirm before keeping it.}

\emph{Recent variants cost more than SMOTE.} CISO reports a median resampling
time of 47 ms per fold against 1.2 ms for SMOTE, about 39 times
\pcite{yadav2025ciso}, and QC-SMOTE about 39.47 times (its Table~7)
\pcite{upman2026qcsmote}. CISO also reports no significant improvement over SMOTE,
class weighting or random oversampling in PR-AUC.
\claudedraft{The move towards safer, more elaborate generators has not yet been
matched by evidence that the extra computation pays off;
Section~\ref{sec:metrics} examines that evidence.}

\emph{The family has a boundary, and it is moving.} Since 2018, nine new
generators in the corpus have left interpolation for spheres, contours, potential
fields or fitted distributions, and deep generative models (CTGAN, SOS, GOIO,
DGOT) learn the minority distribution instead of interpolating between examples.

\section{Which SMOTE-Family Method to Use}
\label{sec:method-choice}

A reader who needs to oversample a training set, rather than to design a new
method, can use the survey to choose among the 37 methods. We select with three
criteria, applied in order of what the corpus can show.

\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{C1, performance on par with SMOTE.} In its own per-dataset comparison
      the method does at least as well as plain SMOTE (median $\Delta\ge 0$ in
      Table~\ref{tab:smote-family}). We do not rank methods by the size of their
      reported gain. Of the 26 papers that report a difference from SMOTE, 19 lie
      within $\pm 0.03$ of it, and only two (AB-SMOTE and RBBoost) are below it;
      the reported differences come from each paper's own protocol and metric, so
      within this band performance does not separate the methods.
\item \emph{C2, a simple method.} The method changes at most two of the six
      decision points, does not run inside a learner, and does not learn its
      generator; 13 of the 37 methods qualify.
\item \emph{C3, no loss of data.} The method keeps every original training
      example and stays within the SMOTE-family definition: it adds interpolated
      points and does not undersample, clean or move existing examples; 23 of the
      37 qualify.
\end{itemize}

Ten methods meet C2 and C3 (Table~\ref{tab:method-choice}); six of them, including
SMOTE, also meet C1 on the evidence their papers print.

\paragraph{Binary only.} Plain SMOTE remains the first choice, as the reference
every variant is measured against. Where a variant is wanted, ADASYN changes only
the allocation of points, keeps every example and scores above SMOTE on all five
of its datasets ($+0.015$), though on little evidence; FW-SMOTE changes only the
distance used to find neighbours and has the broadest support among the simple
variants ($+0.018$, higher on 30 of 42 datasets), but its authors note that it has
many parameters to tune; CGMOS changes only the seed weights ($+0.009$, 24 of 30).
Borderline-SMOTE, Safe-Level-SMOTE, Cluster-SMOTE and G-SMOTE are as simple and
keep all data, but their papers give no per-dataset comparison with SMOTE, so C1
cannot be checked; AB-SMOTE fails C1 ($-0.029$, higher on 2 of 8 datasets).

\paragraph{Multiclass only.} SMOM is the only Multiclass-only method that meets all
three criteria ($+0.004$, higher on 19 of 27 datasets; it changes only how the
partner is chosen). Its authors describe the implementation as complex
\draftnum{(NBDOS clustering and a two-round filtration)}. The simplest alternative
is not a method paper of its own: decomposing the problem one-vs-one and applying
plain SMOTE to each pair of classes, which was among the best combinations in the
study of \pcite{fernandez2013analysing}.

\paragraph{Binary + Multiclass.} RSMOTE meets all three criteria and works in both
settings: it changes only the seeds and the allocation, keeps every example,
oversamples every class to the size of the largest, and scores above SMOTE on 21
of its 27 two-class datasets ($+0.021$). It is the method we recommend when the
same procedure must serve two-class and multiclass data.

The seven methods with the largest reported gains (median $\Delta$ above $0.03$:
SMOTEBoost, SSRBF/DSRBF, GAMO, EMDO, NROMM, MC-MBRC and MKC-SMOTE) all fail C2 or
C3: they run inside a learner, learn their generator, change four or more
decisions, or remove or move original examples. Four of them were also compared
with SMOTE inside a multiclass or ensemble wrapper rather than with plain SMOTE.
\claudedraft{The methods that look strongest on their own papers' numbers are the
ones that change the most, which is what makes their gains hardest to attribute
and to reproduce.}

\section{Recommendations for Describing a New Variant}
\label{sec:method-recommendations}
\begin{enumerate}
\item State which of the six decisions the method changes, and keep the others as
      in SMOTE unless there is a reason not to.
\item When more than one decision changes, report a comparison that changes one
      decision at a time against SMOTE.
\item State the class setting in which the generator is run, and whether any
      multiclass dataset was reduced to two classes and how.
\item State how nominal and mixed features are handled, or that they are not.
\item Report the running time relative to SMOTE on the same data.
\end{enumerate}
